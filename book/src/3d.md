# 3D: meshes, materials and the sun

The same engine draws 3D: the same ECS, systems, clock and tools, with
lit meshes under the 2D layer. Turn it on in `Cargo.toml`:

```toml
[dependencies]
ponko = { version = "0.1", features = ["3d"] }
```

Games that leave it off pay nothing for it. The C library (`ponko_ffi`)
always has it.

The 3D names live in `ponko::d3`: start a 3D file with
`use ponko::prelude::*;` and `use ponko::d3::*;`, then write `Transform`,
`Camera`, `PointLight`, `Text` and so on without a suffix. Meshes,
materials and the other render types are in `ponko::render`, animation in
`ponko::anim` and terrain in `ponko::terrain`. This chapter writes
`d3::Transform` where 2D could be meant; code that uses both dimensions does
the same (the prelude brings the `d2` and `d3` modules). The C and C++
headers keep the suffixed spellings ([Naming and compatibility](naming.md)).

A 3D entity has a `d3::Transform` (where it is, which way it faces, its
size) and a `Model` (which mesh to draw, and a tint). Here's a village of
houses that turn slowly:

## Space

3D space is right-handed with +Y up. The default camera looks along -Z.
Units are yours to choose; meters work well. A `d3::Transform` has a
`position`, a `rotation` (a quaternion, `Quat`) and a `scale`, and is
built the same way as a 2D one:

```rust
ponko::d3::Transform::at(vec3(0.0, 1.0, -5.0))
    .rotated(Quat::from_rotation_y(0.5))
    .scaled(2.0)
```

`t.looking_at(target)` turns it to face a point, and `t.forward()` is the
way it faces (its -Z axis). C++ has `ponko::transform3d_at(position,
rotation, scale)`, `ponko::looking_at`, `ponko::forward`, and `*` on
quaternions and vectors.

## Meshes and materials

A **material** says how a surface looks: its `color` (and an optional
`texture`), how rough it is (0 is a mirror, 1 is chalk), how `metallic`
(0 for most things, 1 for bare metal) and the light it gives off
(`emissive`). Lighting is physically based, as in glTF, so models made in
Blender look the same here.

A **mesh** is triangles with materials. There are three ways to get one:

- **Shapes**: `MeshData::cube(size)`, `plane(size)` and
  `sphere(radius, segments)`, then `ctx.gfx.create_mesh(&data, material)`.
- **Your own triangles**: fill a `MeshData` with positions, indices (three
  per triangle, counter-clockwise from the front) and optionally normals,
  texture coordinates and `colors` (one per corner, multiplying the
  material's color). Normals you leave out are computed. A second set of
  texture coordinates, `uvs1` (glTF's `TEXCOORD_1`), is there for your own
  shaders to read as `v.uv1` and `s.uv1`: texture layers, masks,
  lightmaps. `create_mesh` checks that the data fits together.
- **glTF files**: `ctx.gfx.load_mesh("models/house.glb")` loads a `.glb` (or a
  `.gltf` with its files) from `assets/`, with every mesh in its scene,
  its materials with all their maps (base color, normal, occlusion,
  roughness and metalness, emissive), vertex colors, transparency and
  double-sidedness. Loading the same file again returns the same mesh.

Meshes and materials are cheap handles: make each one once, at startup,
and spawn as many models of it as you like. A `Model` tint
(`Model::new(mesh).with_color(c)`) multiplies the mesh's colors, so a
thousand cubes can come in a thousand colors from one mesh.

### One mesh, several materials

To draw a mesh with another material altogether, add a `ModelMaterial`:
`(d3::Transform::default(), Model::new(rock), ModelMaterial(wet))` draws the
rock's triangles with `wet` instead of the materials it was made or loaded
with, without a second copy of the mesh. Models that share a mesh and a
material are still drawn together. It works on every kind of model: plain
ones, skinned ones (on the entity with the `Skeleton`, or on a model that
borrows one with `SkinOf`), morphing ones, the renderers of a `LodGroup`
and the levels of a `TerrainTile`. A separate `ShadowMesh` keeps its own
materials.

A mesh from a file often has several parts, each with its material (the
walls and the roof of a house). `ModelMaterials::new(&[(1, rust)])` changes
only the parts it lists, numbered from 0 in the order of the file's
materials; the other parts keep the `ModelMaterial`, or without one their
own. (`ctx.gfx.loaded_materials("roof")` finds a file's material by name.) A part
listed twice takes the last, and a part the mesh doesn't have is ignored.

For a mesh whose look always comes from its models,
`ctx.gfx.create_plain_mesh(&data)` makes one with no material of its own: it
is `create_mesh` with `ctx.gfx.default_material()`, plain white.

file and the material's name in it (or its number, when the name is empty
or used twice):

```json
"ModelMaterial": { "mesh": "models/house.glb", "material": "roof" },
"ModelMaterials": { "parts": [

] }
```

Loading the scene loads the named files first. A material made in code
(`create_material`) has no file to name, like a mesh made in code: such an
override is left out of the file, and the game adds it again after loading.

### Animation clips

`ctx.gfx.load_clips(path)` loads every animation in a skinned glTF file.
`ctx.gfx.load_clip(path, name)` selects one, and lists available names if missing.
The `try_load_clips` and `try_load_clip` variants return `Result<_, ponko::error::Error>` instead.
Clips share their data when cloned and are cached with the file. Morph-weight
channels are currently ignored; translation, rotation and scale support Step,
Linear and glTF CubicSpline interpolation.

Sample clips into a skeleton each frame. Time clamps to the clip's endpoints;
use the remainder for looping, guarding zero-duration clips. For a crossfade,
sample the first clip and blend toward the second with a weight from 0 to 1:

`AnimationTrack` stores vector values as xyz with w ignored and quaternion

value, outgoing tangent. Tangents are derivatives per second; cubic rotation
results are normalized. You can keep time and fade weights in your own
components, or use an animator.

`Motion::Blend1D` linearly blends neighbouring sorted thresholds on a float
parameter, clamping beyond the ends. `Motion::Blend2D` uses normalized inverse
squared distance on two floats; an exact child point selects exactly that
child (first wins for coincident points). Trees can contain clips or nested
trees through `MotionChild`, whose positive `time_scale` multiplies seconds.
A tree's duration is the longest child duration divided by its time scale.
Each leaf loops at its own duration for looping states; clamped states hold
leaves at their ends. State speed can also be multiplied by a float parameter;
negative parameter values pause playback. Missing tracks use the rest pose.
Rotations use incremental shortest-path slerp in child order.

Transitions are tested in vector order, first match wins, at most once per
update. All conditions must pass: float greater/less, bool equality, or a set
trigger. Only a selected transition consumes its triggers. An exit time is a
minimum on the source's unwrapped normalized clock, checked against the end of
the update; an any-state transition ignores it and skips its current target.
The target starts at `offset` and advances for the update's full `dt`.
`play` and `crossfade` queue commands for the next update (last command wins),
which override graph transitions for that update.

Fades last a fixed number of seconds, independent of playback speed. Both
states advance during an ordinary fade. An interruption starts from the last
displayed mixed pose and freezes that pose as its source, preserving continuity.
Only the destination state tests transitions and emits events during a fade.
Events use normalized times in `[0, 1)`; zero fires on a loop wrap, not initial
entry. Crossed events, including multiple loop wraps, arrive in time order via
`drain_events`. The reusable buffer retains at most 64 events between drains;
excess events are dropped. Sampling buffers allocate on first update or when
the skeleton layout changes; warm up clips on the target layout before gameplay.

### Layers and root motion

A layer plays its own graph over the base animation, with its own
parameters, clock and events: a wave over a walk, or an additive lean. Its
weight fades it in and out, and a `JointMask` limits it to part of the
skeleton (`JointMask::descendants` picks a joint and everything under it).

Layers apply in the order they were added, each on the pose below it.
`LayerBlend::Override` blends toward the layer's pose by its weight.
`LayerBlend::Additive` adds the layer's difference from a reference pose:
`reference_pose` if you set one, otherwise the layer's current motion at
time zero. Layers update even at weight zero, so they stay in time.

With root motion, the root joint's horizontal movement and turning (yaw)
are taken out of the pose; height, pitch and roll stay in it.
`root_motion()` returns what the latest update took out, in the entity's
own axes. Whole loops count, faster states move further, and crossfades blend
the two states' movement. `apply_root_motion` also moves the entity's
`d3::Transform` by it during the engine's automatic updates;
`extract_root_motion` alone leaves moving the entity to you, for example to
drive a physics character instead. Pick a root joint at the top of the
hierarchy (often the hips): its axes are the ones extraction uses.

### IK and look-at

Register procedural corrections in `Phase::Late`
(`.add(Phase::Late, system)`; C++: `late_system`): it runs once per
frame after automatic animators and before drawing, in registration order,
with frame dt and system timings. It does not run in headless simulations.
`two_bone_ik` rotates a direct three-joint chain toward a world target and
uses a world-space pole direction to choose its bend. Unreachable targets
extend to full reach; weight zero preserves the pose. `look_at_joint` aims a
joint-local axis, with a correction angle limit in radians and a blend weight.
Apply it parent first with separate weights to spread aim over a chain.

Entity non-uniform scale is removed before solving. Exact two-bone reach
requires uniform positive joint scales; non-uniform joint scale and shear
are approximate. Zero-length bones and singular transforms are ignored.
`ctx.draw_skeleton` uses projected 2D lines over the scene: bones are shown
through surfaces, without depth testing.

### Morph targets

A morph target (blend shape) is a named shape a mesh can blend toward: an
offset for each corner, scaled by a weight. Blend a few to make poses that
a model can slide between: a face between a smile and a blink, sliders for
nose length and jaw width, a dent where something hit.

Each entry of `MeshData::morph_targets` has a name, one position offset
per corner and, optionally, one normal offset per corner so the lighting
follows the shape. `ctx.gfx.morph_target(mesh, name)` finds a target's index.
A `MorphWeights` component on a model says how much of each target to
show: 0 for none, 1 for all of it (values outside that range exaggerate
or invert the shape). A model without one shows its mesh's own weights,
which a glTF file can set per mesh. glTF targets are named from the file's
`targetNames` extras, and meshes of one file that use the same name share
one target.

Morph targets combine with skinning: the shape is added first, then the
joints bend the result. A mesh with morph targets is shaped on the GPU in
the same compute pass as skinning, and WebGL2 uses the same CPU fallback.
glTF `weights` channels animate named targets with STEP, LINEAR or
CUBICSPLINE interpolation, including morph-only files without joints.
`AnimationClip::with_morph_tracks` adds scalar tracks in the x component;
C++ uses `clip::set_morph_track`. One means full weight. The animator
blends these tracks through blend trees, crossfades and override layers.
Additive layers add their weighted morph values (zero reference), independently
of the layer's joint reference pose. Joint masks affect joints only: morph
tracks on a masked layer still run. Clips leave unanimated weights untouched.
Automatic animators write `MorphWeights` on their model and `SkinOf`
attachments. After manual Rust sampling, call `ctx.gfx.write_animated_morphs(&mut ctx.world)`;
C++ `sample_clip` and `animator_update` write the component immediately.
`Skeleton::write_morph_weights` can instead write to a caller-owned weight list.
Partial overrides use the model's initial weights as their baseline; explicit
changes to its weight component establish a new baseline.

Unity/FBX channels can have several authored frames. Importing preserves every
frame, including position and normal deltas. `MeshData::morph_frames` (C++
`mesh_data::add_morph_frame`) links existing targets to a controlling channel
index and a positive frame weight. Set only the channel's weight: halfway may
be its own authored shape, rather than half the final deltas. Evaluation is
piecewise linear, interpolating the first frame from zero and extrapolating
outside the authored range using the end segments. The CPU expands each
channel into at most two active target weights for the existing GPU/CPU
skinning paths. Ordinary glTF targets need no frame metadata.

The Unity importer carries mappings in mesh extras `ponkoMorphFrames`
(`channel`, `target`, `weight` indices/weight), alongside standard glTF targets.
Plain YAML `blendShape.<name>` curves on SkinnedMeshRenderer use 0–100 units
and export sampled weight channels, just like imported transform curves;
Hermite curves are sampled at the clip rate plus source key times. All-step
curves retain STEP interpolation; mixed interpolation is approximated by
linear samples. Animation extras `ponkoAnimatedTargets` identify the sparse
Unity channels so unrelated target weights remain untouched. Compressed or
binary Unity clips remain unsupported.

## Scene settings

Every setting for how the 3D world is drawn lives in one place,
`ctx.scene3d`: the `camera`, the `lighting` (sun and shadows), the `sky`
(and fog), `post` (post-processing, resolution, antialiasing), `lod`
(level-of-detail switching) and `passes` (your own compute passes, effects
and extra cameras). Each is a plain struct you change directly; the next
frame draws with the new values. [Graphics quality](#graphics-quality)
presets and automatic quality are methods on `ctx.scene3d` that write those
same fields. As separate fields, they can be borrowed together:

```rust
# use ponko::prelude::*;
# fn f(ctx: &mut Ctx) {
let s = &mut ctx.scene3d;
s.lighting.shadow_distance = s.camera.far * 0.25; // shadows over the nearest quarter
# }
```

## Camera

`ctx.scene3d.camera` is the 3D camera. It has a `position` and `rotation`, and
`look_at(target)` turns it to face a point. Its `fov_y` is the vertical
field of view in radians (60° by default), and `near` and `far` bound
what's drawn. `ctx.scene3d.camera.screen_ray(mouse)` gives the ray under a
window point, for picking things with the mouse.

C++: `ctx.camera3d()` and `ctx.set_camera3d(c)` read and write it,
`ctx.camera3d_look_at(target)` turns it, and `ctx.screen_ray(point)`
returns the ray.

## Light and shadows

`ctx.scene3d.lighting` holds the sun, the strength of the sky's light, and
the shadows:

- `sun_direction`: which way sunlight travels. Straight down is noon;
  a low angle gives long shadows.
- `sun_color` and `sun_intensity`: the sun's color and brightness.
- `ambient_intensity`: how strong the soft light from the sky is. Where it
  comes from depends on the [sky](#sky-and-fog): the default atmosphere works
  it out from the sun; a flat sky (`ctx.scene3d.sky.atmosphere = false`)
  takes it from three colors on `ctx.scene3d.sky`. Either is also what
  [sky IBL](#sky-ibl-and-local-reflections) lights surfaces with.
  - `sky_color` and `ground_color`: light from above and below.
  - `equator_color` (`None` by default): light from the horizon. With it the
    ambient goes from the ground color below, through this on the horizon,
    to the sky color above, so walls facing sideways get a color of their
    own (a three-color gradient, as other engines' "trilight" ambient).

- `shadows` and `shadow_distance`: the sun casts soft shadows up to that
  far from the camera (60 units by default), fading out at the edge.
- `shadow_cascades` (1 to 4, default 4): how many shadow maps share that
  distance. Each covers a stretch further out than the one before, so
  shadows stay sharp near the camera even when they reach hundreds of
  units; neighbouring cascades blend where they meet. Fewer are cheaper:
  each one draws its shadow casters again.
- `shadow_resolution` (256 to 4096, default 2048): the width of each
  cascade's shadow map. The maps take `4 × resolution² × cascades` bytes of
  GPU memory (64 MB by default). Change it while the game runs and the
  maps are made again at the new size.
- `shadow_refresh` (1 to 8, default 2): how often, in frames, the far half
  of the cascades is drawn again. The far cascades take turns, so at 2 each
  one is drawn every other frame and the far shadows cost half as much.
  Each is drawn into a box a little bigger than it needs (texels about 20%
  coarser), so the camera can move a while before it's drawn again early;
  so is a cascade whose box the camera leaves, and every cascade when the sun
  moves. What moves out there casts a shadow that lags by up to that many
  frames. 1 draws every cascade every frame, in a tight box.
- `shadow_filter` (`ShadowFilter::Sharp`, `Soft` or `Softest`, the
  default): how soft the sun's shadow edges are. Each lit pixel looks the
  shadow up once (`Sharp`: a smooth 2×2 texel edge), four times (`Soft`:
  3×3) or nine times (`Softest`: 4×4). On a large, high-resolution screen
  the lookups are a good part of drawing every lit pixel, so `Sharp` or
  `Soft` is a cheap win on weaker GPUs.

The shadow maps follow the camera and move in whole texels, so shadow
edges stay still while the camera moves.

A shadow map (a cascade, or a point or spot light's view) that would be
drawn exactly as it was last time is kept instead: nothing it covers moved,
appeared or went, and its light, its box and the shadow settings stayed the
same. A still scene with a still camera draws no shadow casters at all, and
a moving model redraws only the maps that see it. Shadows look the same
either way. Maps that see skinned or morphing models, buffer-driven meshes,
or materials whose shader moves vertices itself (or cuts out pixels with its
own `surface`) are drawn every frame, as their shadows can change without
anything the engine sees changing.

Only casters whose shadow can land on something the camera sees (out to
`shadow_distance`) go into the cascades: a tree behind the camera with
the sun in front of it is skipped. Casters smaller than one of a
cascade's texels are left out of it too, so pebbles cast in the near
cascades and not in the far ones, where their shadow wouldn't show.

Every model casts a shadow, and each one costs as much in the shadow maps
as on screen. For small things that crowd the ground, such as grass and
pebbles, turn it off per mesh with `ctx.gfx.set_mesh_shadows(mesh, false)`:

Per entity, a `ShadowMesh` picks what goes into the shadow maps instead of
the model: a simplified copy or a billboard for a detailed model far away
(a shadow there covers a few texels, so a tenth of the triangles casts the
same one), or `ShadowMesh(None)` for no shadow at all. An entity with a
`ShadowMesh` and no `Model` casts only a shadow: one simple stand-in for
the shadows of many small pieces, such as a fence, that cast none
themselves. On a `TerrainTile`, `ShadowMesh(None)` turns the ground's own
shadow off. Scene files write it as `"ShadowMesh": { "mesh": "file.glb" }`
or `{ "mesh": null }`.

Shadows are often where a big 3D scene's GPU time goes: every caster is
drawn once per shadow cascade it falls in, also when it's behind the
camera. The [profiling tools](performance.md#profiling-a-frame) count the
triangles drawn into the shadow maps separately from the camera's.

## Normal maps, ORM maps and glow

Besides `texture`, a `MaterialDesc` takes the maps that glTF models come
with:

- `normal_map`: surface detail (bumps, grooves, bricks) as tangent-space
  normals, OpenGL style. `normal_strength` scales it.
- `orm_map`: ambient occlusion in red, roughness in green and metallic in
  blue, multiplying `roughness` and `metallic`.
- `emissive_map`: multiplies `emissive`.

A texture gets mipmaps (smaller copies of itself) the first time a
material uses it, so it stays smooth far away and at a slant instead of
shimmering. Sprites drawing the same texture still use it at full size.

Material textures take a quarter of the video memory or less as
[compressed textures](drawing.md#compressed-textures): `.ktx2` files that
`ponko textures` makes from your images, with their mipmaps made ahead of
time. Mark normal maps `normal` and ORM maps `linear` in its
`textures.json`; a BC5 normal map keeps X and Y, and the shader rebuilds Z.

Materials are solid by default (`Blend::Opaque`), whatever their alpha.
`blend: Blend::Cutout` cuts out the pixels whose alpha is below 0.5
(leaves, fences, grass cards), in their shadows too. The edge of the cut
is smoothed with the multisampling (alpha to coverage): the alpha is
sharpened to a ramp about a pixel wide around 0.5, and each pixel keeps as
many of its samples as that says, so leaves and wire fences don't stair-step
or shimmer. Shadows keep the hard cut. It costs more, as the
material's shader then also runs for hidden pixels and shadows, so keep it
for materials with holes. `blend: Blend::Transparent` makes a material
see-through by its alpha (glass, water). Transparent models are drawn after everything solid and
cast no shadows; they aren't sorted, so overlapping ones can show in the
wrong order. `double_sided: true` draws the back of each triangle too
(leaves, cloth). `ctx.gfx.update_material(material, desc)` changes a material
while the game runs.

## Point and spot lights

Attach a `d3::PointLight` or `d3::SpotLight` to an entity with a `d3::Transform`.
Point lights shine in every direction; spots aim along the transform's local
**−Z** (`forward()`). Transform scale never changes their range. Color is sRGB
(alpha ignored); intensity is linear radiant intensity. Cone angles are **half
angles in radians**, with `0 <= inner_angle <= outer_angle < pi/2`.

Both types use the sun's Cook–Torrance GGX diffuse/specular model.
Distance attenuation is `max(1 - (distance/range)^4, 0)^2 / max(distance^2, 1)`:
finite near the source, smoothly zero at the range. Spots also smoothstep from
the inner cone to the outer cone; equal angles give a hard edge.
Nonfinite or nonpositive intensity/range and invalid cone angles disable a light.

The CPU culls lights conservatively and bins them into 16×9 screen tiles with
24 logarithmic depth slices, independently for each camera (including target
and reflection cameras). Pixels loop over their cluster's list, with no fixed
per-cluster cap or silent overflow. CPU lists and GPU textures retain capacity
and grow only at new high-water marks. Allocation-free steady state refers to
the engine's light collection and packing; the GPU driver's upload machinery
is outside that guarantee. Packed textures also work on WebGL2. Adapter texture
limits bound the maximum number of visible lights and cluster references;
exceeding them reports an explicit capacity error.

### Shadows from point and spot lights

Set `shadows: true` on a light (the spot light above has it) to have
models block it. A spot light takes one shadow view, looking down its cone,
and a point light six, one per cube face. Shadowed lights nearest the
camera get theirs first, up to `light_shadow_views` views a frame (12 by
default, at most `d3::PointLight::MAX_SHADOW_VIEWS`, 24); any left over shine
unshadowed. So keep shadows for the lights that need them: a desk lamp,
headlights, a torch, a muzzle flash.

Every view is a square tile of one depth atlas, packed again each frame,
so many small far lights fit beside a few large near ones. A light's tiles
are as wide as it looks big: one whose reach fills the view (or that the
camera stands in) gets `light_shadow_resolution` texels (default
`d3::PointLight::SHADOW_SIZE`, 1024), one twice as far away half that, down to 64.
Set a light's `shadow_resolution` to pick its size yourself (64 to 2048).
Sizes are powers of two; when the views don't all fit the atlas, the
largest shrink first, and only if even the smallest don't fit are the
farthest lights left unshadowed.

## Custom shaders

A material can have its own shader, in [WGSL](https://www.w3.org/TR/WGSL/).
It doesn't have to do everything: it defines one or both of two functions,
and the engine does the rest (lighting, shadows, the depth prepass,
and transparency):

- `fn vertex(v: Vertex) -> Vertex` moves vertices, in world space: wind
  in grass, waves on water. `v.origin` is where the model is, so each
  model can sway or vary on its own.
- `fn surface(s: Surface) -> Surface` changes each pixel: its `albedo`,
  `alpha`, `normal`, `roughness`, `metallic`, `occlusion` and `emissive`,
  filled in from the material first. It can read `s.world`, `s.uv`,
  `s.uv1` (the mesh's second texture coordinates), `s.color` (the model's
  tint times the vertex color), `s.view` (towards the camera) and
  `s.origin` (where the model is: a height above its base, a color per
  model).

Transparent materials can also see what is behind them, for water,
glass and heat haze. With `s.screen` (the pixel's position on screen, in
pixels):

- `scene_color(screen)` is the lit color of the opaque scene there;
- `scene_position(screen)` is the world position of the opaque surface
  there, and `scene_distance(screen)` its distance from the camera.

Offsetting `screen` bends what shows through (refraction), and comparing
`scene_distance(s.screen)` with the surface's own distance gives how
deep the water is. A frame with such a shader copies the scene once
before its transparent models are drawn; frames without one don't pay
for it.

Read the scene through these helpers. The depth texture behind them,
`scene_depth_texture`, is multisampled except in a browser that has only
WebGL2, where the 3D frame has one sample and it is an ordinary depth texture:
`textureLoad(scene_depth_texture, texel, 0)` works in both (the last argument
is a sample index in one and a mip level in the other), and no other value of
it does. On OpenGL (WebGL2, and native OpenGL ES) the engine binds it as a
float texture instead, because WebGL2's shading language can't load a texel
of a depth texture: it reads it through a non-filtering sampler there and
rewrites each `textureLoad(scene_depth_texture, ..)` in your shader to that
read, which returns the same depth. OpenGL can't sample the depth the
transparent pass tests against either, so there that pass reads a copy, made
by one full-screen pass in frames with a scene-reading material.

`ctx.gfx.load_shader("shaders/water.wgsl")` loads one from `assets/`; with
hot reload on, saving the file changes the running game. `cargo run
--release --example shaders --features 3d` shows a lake, grass in the
wind and a pattern made in a shader.

### Shader errors and reloads

A shader that doesn't compile is an error with the compiler's message,
pointing at the line and column in your own file (not in the engine's
code the shader is put together with): `load_shader` and `create_shader`
panic with it, `try_*` and the C API return it. With hot reload on, a
*file* that doesn't compile at load is not fatal: you get its handle, it
draws with a stand-in (the built-in look for a material or
[sprite](drawing.md#blend-modes-and-sprite-shaders) shader, the frame
as it is for a screen effect; a compute pass is skipped), its file stays
watched, and saving a fix loads it. When a reload has an error, the
message is printed and the last good version stays in use.

`ctx.gfx.shader_status()` has all of this as data for each shader, material
shaders, screen effects, compute passes and sprite shaders alike: whether its last
compile or reload worked (`ShaderOutcome`: `Compiled`, `Reloaded`,
`Failed`, `ReloadFailed`), how many edits were reloaded, the frame, and
for an error a `ShaderDiagnostic` with the line and column in your WGSL,
the cause, the compiler's notes, the line of code and the GPU variant
that was compiled. A tool or test can check there that an edit took
effect without reading the log; the `shader_status` MCP tool and the
`shaders.status` control method return the same.

What is checked, and where:

- **The compiler** (naga) checks every shader on every platform, the
  web included, before anything reaches the GPU. Most mistakes stop here,
  with a line and column. An error in the engine's code around yours
  (usually an unclosed brace, or a `vertex`, `surface`, `effect` or
  `compute` function with the wrong signature) says so
  (`in_engine_code`) and gives the line in the composed shader.
- **The GPU** checks the pipelines when they're built: a binding no
  layout has, a limit the device lacks. These errors have no line. Each
  shader's pipelines are built in a GPU error scope, so a rejected one is
  an error (`the GPU rejected its pipelines: ...`) instead of breaking
  the device. On native GPUs and WebGL2 the answer comes at once: the
  new pipelines are never used, and on a reload the last good ones stay.
- **In a browser with WebGPU** the GPU answers a frame or so later. The
  new pipelines are used meanwhile; if the GPU rejects them, the next
  frame puts the previous ones back (or the stand-in, for a new shader),
  prints the error and records it in `shader_status` (`Failed`, or
  `ReloadFailed` with the reload no longer counted). In the frames in
  between, the browser may report a validation error in its console and
  skip drawing.
- **Not caught anywhere**: material shader variants built later, the
  first time a draw needs them (crossfading LOD levels, buffer-driven
  instances; `ctx.gfx.warm_render_pipelines()` builds them early), and a
  screen effect's render target pipeline, built the first time it runs.
  They come from the same checked code, so they rarely fail; if one does
  on the web, the browser reports it in its console. Errors while a frame
  runs (out of memory, a lost device) are not shader errors and aren't
  reported here. On the web, hot reload is off (there are no files to
  watch).

Each shader is one WGSL file: there's no `#include` or `#import` for
your own shader code, so a line number always refers to the file the
shader came from (or to the composed shader when `in_engine_code` is
set).

The renderer builds each material shader into pipelines once and keeps
them in a cache keyed by the shader's code, the GPU features composed
into it, the target formats and the sample count. Shaders made from the
same code share one set of pipelines and compile once; a draw that needs
a variant not built yet (a crossfade, buffer-driven instances) builds it
the first time; and a hot reload throws away only the reloaded shader's
pipelines, so every other shader keeps drawing with what it has. A failed
reload throws away nothing. `ctx.gfx.pipeline_stats()` shows what the cache
did: shaders and pipelines alive, hits and misses, how many compiles and
builds there were and how long the slowest took, and how many pipeline
sets reloads invalidated:

```rust

# fn f(ctx: &mut Ctx) {
let p = ctx.gfx.pipeline_stats();
println!(
    "{} pipelines, {} misses, slowest compile {:.1} ms, {} invalidated",
    p.pipelines, p.misses, p.slowest_compile_ms, p.invalidated
);

```

The same numbers are in `perf_report`'s `render_inspector.pipelines`.

Custom material hooks can call `punctual_light(s: Surface) -> vec3<f32>`.
It returns linear HDR diffuse plus specular using the surface's world position,
normal, view direction, albedo, metallic and roughness. The built-in `shade`
already adds it; adding it to emissive adds the light a second time. For stylized
uses, copy the surface, change its BRDF fields, and evaluate that copy. The
lower-level `direct_light(s, direction_to_light, radiance)` evaluates the same
BRDF for one light. `sunlight(world, normal)` returns sun shadow visibility
(screen effects and compute passes can call it as well).
It includes light shadows for lights that cast them.

### The shader contract

What a material shader can rely on, and what it can't change.

**One source, every pass.** The engine compiles the shader once as three
parts: its prelude (the bindings, `Vertex`, `Surface` and the helpers above),
the game's code, then its own entry points. A shader without `vertex` or
`surface` gets one that returns its input unchanged. The two hooks must have
exactly the signatures `fn vertex(v: Vertex) -> Vertex` and
`fn surface(s: Surface) -> Surface` (parameter names may differ, and a
trailing comma is allowed). Wrong signatures and engine-owned binding
declarations produce a diagnostic naming the hook, expected rule and fix
at the user's file and line. Hooks are ordinary functions: do not add
`@vertex`, `@fragment` or `@compute` entry-point attributes. The same code runs in every pass and variant: there
are no preprocessor defines. Code that must differ in the shadow maps asks
`drawing_shadows()`, and code that picks its own last-frame values in the
motion pass asks `drawing_last_frame()` (see "Motion of `vertex`" below).

**Bindings.** Every group belongs to the engine; a shader reads them by name
and declares none of its own (its own `@group`/`@binding` would clash).

| Group | What | Names |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | the frame | `frame` (the `Frame` uniform), the sun's and lights' shadow maps, the sky, the point and spot light lists; projected decals and global illumination where the GPU has the texture slots |
| 1 | the material | `material` (`color`, `emissive`, `params`, `maps`, `custom[0..4]`), `material_sampler`, `base_texture`, `normal_texture`, `orm_texture`, `emissive_texture`, `custom_texture0..1`, `custom_array0..2` |
| 2 | the scene behind | read through `scene_color`, `scene_position`, `scene_distance` (transparent materials only) |
| 3 | GPU buffer | `items: array<vec4<f32>>` for a mesh drawn from a GPU buffer (see [GPU buffers and particles](#gpu-buffers-and-particles)) |

**Inputs.** `vertex` gets each vertex in world space: `position`, `normal`,
`uv`, `uv1`, `color` (tint times vertex color), `origin` (read-only: changing
it has no effect) and `instance_index` (the element of `items`, for a GPU
buffer mesh). Skinned meshes arrive already posed. `surface` gets the
material's values with its textures applied, and the read-only `world`, `uv`,
`uv1`, `color`, `view`, `screen` and `origin`; the normal is already flipped
on the back of a double-sided surface. The engine clamps `roughness` to 0.03–1
and `metallic` to 0–1 after `surface`.

**Which hook runs where.**

| Pass | `vertex` | `surface` | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Depth prepass | yes | cut-outs only, for alpha | solid surfaces run no fragment shader there |
| Opaque | yes | yes | lit; decals applied after `surface` |
| Transparent | yes | yes | blended by `alpha`; may read the scene |
| Shadow maps | yes | cut-outs only, for alpha | `drawing_shadows()` is true; transparent surfaces cast none |
| Motion vectors (temporal antialiasing, temporal AO) | yes, twice | no | once as of this frame; once with last frame's transform, pose, `frame.time` and kept `items` |
| Global illumination probes | yes | yes | lit like the opaque pass |

Because the depth, shadow and lit passes all run the same `vertex`, moved
geometry casts shadows where it is drawn and the prepass agrees with it.

**Motion of `vertex`.** The motion-vector pass runs `vertex` twice, and the
difference is the pixel's motion: once as in the other passes, once as of the
last drawn frame. In that second run the model's last transform and pose come
in, `frame.time` reads the last frame's time (`frame.time.zw`, so
`frame.time.x` is the last frame's seconds), and `items[i]` reads element
`i` as it was, for a buffer that keeps its last frame
(`set_gpu_buffer_motion`, see [GPU buffers and
particles](#gpu-buffers-and-particles)). Wind, waves and particles therefore
write their own motion. The engine switches every direct `frame.time` and
`items[..]` read in the game's code, in `vertex` and in the functions it
calls, with the lines unchanged, so errors still point at them; through a
pointer (`&frame.time`) they stay this frame's. Other inputs (`material`
values, textures) are the same in both runs: a shader that animates with
them can ask `drawing_last_frame()`, true only in the second run, and pick
its own last-frame value (`material.custom` can carry both). On the first
frame, after `reset_temporal_history()` and on the frame TAA is turned on,
the last frame's time is this frame's: no motion. `reset_motion(entity)`
drops the model's transform and pose motion, not what its `vertex` adds.

**Variants.** Each shader is built as six pipeline sets, picked per draw;
none changes the shader's code, only how it is drawn:

| Variant | Chosen by | What changes |
|---|---|---|
| one-sided / double-sided | `MaterialDesc::double_sided` | back faces culled, or drawn with the flipped normal |
| plain instances | every ordinary or instanced model | — |
| crossfading instances | a model fading between [levels of detail](#levels-of-detail) | every pass dithers its pixels |
| shared-instance draws | a mesh drawn from a [GPU buffer](#gpu-buffers-and-particles) | all instances read one model record (no crossfade) |

Inside each set, the blend picks the pipelines: `Blend::Opaque` writes depth
without the shader's fragment part (or with it on GPUs that skip the solid
prepass), `Blend::Cutout` runs `surface` in its depth and shadow passes and
uses alpha to coverage for soft edges, and `Blend::Transparent` draws last,
blended and without depth or shadows. Plain sets are built with the shader;
the others the first time a frame needs them, or all while loading with
`warm_pipelines`. There is no separate unlit or skinned variant: skinned
meshes are posed before drawing and use the same pipelines, and an unlit look
is explicitly selected by setting `s.unlit = true` in `surface`. It returns
`albedo + emissive`, without direct light, specular, ambient, GI or shadows
received. Textures, tint, alpha, decals, fog and tone mapping still apply.
The default is `false`; emissive alone does not disable specular lighting.
Copy the input (`var out = s`) to retain the other fields. Full positional
WGSL `Surface(...)` constructors must include the new boolean after
`emissive`; Rust and native material descriptors retain their layouts.

**Supported feature combinations.** All Cartesian combinations in the first
four rows are supported; lighting never changes alpha or pass selection.

| Feature | Supported values | Working sample |
|---|---|---|
| Alpha | opaque (ignores alpha), cutout (threshold / MSAA coverage), transparent (premultiplied blend) | `material_features` below |
| Faces | one-sided, double-sided (flipped back-face normal) | `material_features` |
| Lighting | lit (default), explicit unlit (`Surface.unlit`) | `material_unlit` |
| Geometry | ordinary entities, skinned entities, ordinary instanced entities, static persistent batches | `material_skin`, `material_instances` |
| LOD | plain or crossfading entities; persistent distance bands select one level | [Levels of detail](#levels-of-detail) |
| GPU-buffer instances | shared model record, per-element `items` in `vertex`, on storage-capable devices; a shader reading `items` otherwise fails with a located fix, and unsupported setup panics with a fix to use CPU-backed batches/entities | [GPU buffers and particles](#gpu-buffers-and-particles) |
| Skinned + static persistent batch or GPU-buffer draw | unsupported: creation panics with a fix; use `Model` entities with `Skeleton` or `SkinOf` | animated poses require entities |
| Shared-instance + crossfade | shared wins; no crossfade by design | buffer-driven draws never crossfade |

A transparent surface deliberately has no depth, shadow, GI-capture or
velocity draw. Cutout alpha is **not** evaluated in velocity: vectors use
the depth left by the visible surface. Depth and shadow `surface` inputs
have the same interpolated world position, UVs, vertex tint, normal and
material values as color shading. `view` is
`normalize(frame.camera.xyz - world)` using that pass's frame uniform;
use `toward_viewer(world)` for a shadow-aware viewer direction. `screen`
is in that pass's target pixels. Do not read scene-behind helpers there; those
are only meaningful in transparent color shading. Vertex inputs are the
posed world-space values in every pass; in the motion pass's second run they
are the last frame's (see "Motion of `vertex`" above).

The renderer builds one pipeline set per material shader for each
combination of faces (one- or double-sided) and instancing (plain,
crossfading, GPU-buffer shared): six sets. A shader whose `vertex` reads
`frame.time`, `items` or `drawing_last_frame()` (directly or through its
own functions) gets one more pipeline in each set, to draw its models'
motion while they hold still; other shaders don't. Blend chooses a role inside the
set; skinned and unlit do not add sets. Shader identity, target formats,
sample counts and device features are separate parts of the pipeline cache.

## Sky and fog

`ctx.scene3d.sky` draws a physical sky behind the 3D world, lit by the sun in
`ctx.scene3d.lighting`: blue by day, orange and red when the sun is low, dark blue
with stars at night. The same air lights the scene: the sun turns orange
and dims as it sets, and the sky's light (bluish in the shade) lights the
scene. One world unit counts as a meter.

- `atmosphere`: on by default, and it draws the whole background. Off, the
  sky is flat: the background is the clear color (`ctx.gfx.clear`), and
  the ambient light comes from `sky_color`, `ground_color` and
  `equator_color` (see [Light and shadows](#light-and-shadows)). Those
  three are used only by the flat sky, like `fog_color`.
- `haze`: dust and water in the air. More gives a paler sky, a bigger
  glow around the sun and redder sunsets.
- `sun_size` (degrees across), `stars` and `night_light` (moonlight and
  starlight, when the sun is down).
- `fog_density`, `fog_height` and `fog_falloff`: height fog, thickest at
  `fog_height` and thinning out above it. It takes the sky's color behind
  it (or `fog_color` without an atmosphere) and covers the sky near the
  horizon too.
- `fog_start` and `fog_end`: distance fog, none closer than `fog_start`
  and all fog past `fog_end`, whatever the height. Off while `fog_end`
  isn't past `fog_start` (the default). It takes the same color as the
  height fog, and leaves the sky alone.
- `fog_sun_glow` and `fog_sun_focus`: sunlight the fog scatters toward
  the camera, so both kinds glow when looking toward the sun; the focus
  (0 to under 1) draws the glow in close around it.

`Sky::sun_direction_at(hour)` gives the sun's direction for a time of
day, for a day and night cycle: it rises in the east (+X) at 6, is
highest at noon and sets in the west at 18.

## Screen effects

A screen effect is a shader run over the whole lit 3D frame, for looks
that belong to the picture rather than to one surface: under water, heat
haze, a dream blur, sun shafts, outlines. It defines one function:

- `fn effect(p: Pixel) -> vec3<f32>` gives the new light at each pixel,
  from `p.color` (the light there so far, brighter than white where the
  scene is bright), `p.world` and `p.distance` (the nearest solid
  surface and how far it is from the camera), `p.sky` (nothing solid
  there), `p.screen` (in pixels) and `p.uv` (0 to 1 across the screen).

It can read other pixels with `scene_color(screen)` and
`scene_position(screen)`, and everything a material shader reads:
`frame`, the noise helpers, and `material.custom` and the textures of the
material it's given. That includes the sun's shadow maps:
`sunlight(world, normal)` says how much sun reaches a point (0 in shadow,
1 lit), so an effect can march along the view ray and add light only where
the sun gets through, for sun shafts in fog or dust. Compute passes can
call it too. Make one with `ctx.gfx.create_effect` or
`ctx.gfx.load_effect` (hot reloaded, like material shaders), and run it by
adding a `ScreenEffect` to `ctx.scene3d.passes.effects`. Effects run in that order, each
on what the one before left, after the transparent models and before
bloom and the post-processing below. `p.world` is the nearest *solid*
surface: transparent models don't count.

## Render targets

A render target is a texture a second camera draws the world into, each
frame, before the main view: for mirrors, water that reflects the hills,
a security camera's monitor, a portal. `ctx.gfx.create_render_target(w, h)`
makes one, and a `TargetCamera` in `ctx.scene3d.passes.target_cameras` draws into it.
Materials then use it like any other texture. It holds the lit scene with
its sky and fog, in the same HDR light as the frame before tone mapping
(so put it in `emissive`, not `albedo`, to show it as it is). Target
cameras use the main view's shadows and don't draw transparent models.

`TargetCamera::reflection(camera, target, level)` is the camera for a
level mirror at height `level`: `camera` mirrored below it, drawing only
what's above it (its `clip_plane`, which tilts the camera's near plane
onto the mirror, so it costs nothing). Read it at the screen position
flipped upside down, and update it every frame as the camera moves:

## Compute passes

A compute pass runs a shader on the GPU once per texel of a render
target, every frame, before anything is drawn (or, as a scene compute
pass, once the frame is lit: see [Custom passes](#custom-passes)): for things that live in a
texture and change over time, such as ripples, footprints and tyre tracks
in snow or mud, wet ground drying, spreading fire, or fog of war. It
defines one function:

- `fn compute(c: Cell) -> vec4<f32>` gives the texel's new value, from
  what it holds now (`c.value`, all 0 at first), where it is (`c.pixel`,
  `c.size`, and `c.uv` from 0 to 1) and its neighbours:
  `cell_at(pixel)` reads any texel as it was before this pass.

It also reads `frame` (`frame.time.y` is the frame's seconds) and the
values and textures of the material it's given, as screen effects do, so
the game steers it with `ctx.gfx.update_material`. Make one with
`ctx.gfx.create_compute` or `ctx.gfx.load_compute` (hot reloaded), and run it by
adding a `ComputePass` to `ctx.scene3d.passes.compute`. Passes run in that order;
the same pass twice steps the simulation twice a frame. Materials read the
target as a texture, in the same frame:

## Custom passes

A game adds its own GPU work to a 3D frame at a few named stages, each a
list in `Ctx`. There is no render graph to build and no plugin to
register: push an entry onto the stage's list, and it runs every frame,
in list order, until you take it off. In the order the frame runs them:

| Stage | List | Runs | Its shader sees |
|---|---|---|---|
| compute | `ctx.scene3d.passes.compute` | before anything is drawn | no scene (`scene_color` is black) |
| target cameras | `ctx.scene3d.passes.target_cameras` | before the main view | the world, from its camera |
| scene compute | `ctx.scene3d.passes.scene_compute` | after the opaque and transparent models and temporal antialiasing | this frame's lit image and depth |
| target effects | `ctx.scene3d.passes.target_effects` | right after the scene compute passes | this frame's lit image and depth |
| screen effects | `ctx.scene3d.passes.effects` | last, before bloom and tone mapping | the frame so far |

A scene compute pass is a `ComputePass` like the ones above (one shader
for both stages; texture or GPU buffer), but it runs once the lit frame is
settled and its shader can call `scene_color(pixel)`, `scene_position(pixel)`
and `scene_distance(pixel)`, where `frame.screen.xy` is the frame's size in
pixels. Unlike a target effect, it keeps its target from frame to frame
(`c.value`) and can write a GPU buffer: a light meter for eye adaptation,
a map of what the camera has seen, particles that bounce off the depth.
`examples/scene_compute.rs` measures the frame's light into a 1x1 target
and a screen effect evens the exposure out with it:

```rust
    fn compute(c: Cell) -> vec4<f32> {

        return vec4<f32>(mix(c.value.r, now, 0.05), 0.0, 0.0, 1.0);
```

**Who sees what.** Within a frame, a pass that reads a target sees the
result of every pass before it, and last frame's result of the passes
after it (all 0 at first). So a screen effect sampling a scene compute
pass's target sees this frame's value; a material sampling it sees last
frame's, because the models are drawn before the scene stage.

**Resources.** Each entry declares what it writes (its render target or
GPU buffer) and what it reads (the textures of its material, plus the
scene at the later stages). The engine checks those declarations every
frame against the rules the renderer's own passes follow, and skips an
entry that would break one, printing once which entry (its list, its
index and its shader) and which render target:

- A pass can't sample the texture it renders to: a target effect whose
  material samples its own target is skipped.
- A target camera or a target effect replaces all of its target, so it
  can't come after another writer of that target in the same frame: the
  first result would be lost. The target effect is skipped (or, under a
  target camera, the compute pass). Compute passes update their target in
  place, so several of them on one target (in either compute stage, or a
  scene compute pass after a target camera) are a chain, not a hazard.

GPU buffers are always updated in place, in stage and list order.

**Feature gating.** Compute passes of both stages need compute shaders
(`GpuFeature::Compute` in [What this GPU can do](#what-this-gpu-can-do)).
Where they're missing (WebGL2, or `PONKO_GPU_TIER=webgl2`),
`try_create_compute` returns an error for a texture pass, buffer passes do
nothing, and the rest of the frame draws as without them: check the
result and fall back, as the example does. Target effects and screen
effects run everywhere.

**Cost.** A frame with all these lists empty records none of their steps
and skips the per-frame check, so the standard path pays nothing for
them. Each scene compute pass costs one dispatch (and, for a texture, one
copy of its target), and keeps the scene's depth stored after the opaque
pass, which a GPU that draws in tiles may otherwise skip.

**From C and C++.** `ponko_ctx_set_scene_compute_passes` and
`ponko_ctx_scene_compute_passes` (C++ `set_scene_compute_passes`,
`scene_compute_passes`) take the same `PonkoComputePass` as the compute
stage. `ponko_ctx_set_target_effects` and `ponko_ctx_target_effects` take
`PonkoTargetEffect` (an effect, the render target it writes and an optional
material); in C++, `ponko::target_effect_of(effect, target)` or
`target_effect_of(effect, target, material)` makes one for
`set_target_effects`. A list with a plain texture as a target, or an unknown
shader or material, is refused and leaves the stage as it was.

## Post-processing

The 3D frame is lit in HDR: light can be brighter than white. On its way
to the screen, `ctx.scene3d.post` decides how it looks:

- `exposure`: brightness in stops (+1 doubles it).
- `bloom` and `bloom_threshold`: light brighter than the threshold glows
  into its surroundings (0 turns bloom off).
- `temperature` and `tint`: white balance, cooler or warmer, greener or
  pinker.
- `contrast` and `saturation`: 1 keeps them as they are.
- `tonemap`: how bright light rolls off to white. `Tonemap::Aces` (the
  default) is film-like, `AgX` keeps the hue of bright colors,
  `Neutral` stays closest to the materials' own colors.
- `lut`: a color lookup table for grading in an image editor. Start from
  `Post::neutral_lut(32)`, save it as a PNG, paint over it, and load it
  with `ctx.load_texture`.
- `vignette`: darker corners.

### Ambient occlusion and post antialiasing

`ctx.scene3d.post.ambient_occlusion` enables GTAO: screen-space horizon integration
with normals reconstructed from depth, followed by a depth-aware bilateral
filter. Only ambient/indirect light is occluded; direct sun, punctual lights
and emissive surfaces retain their light. Radius is in world units. Start
with radius 1, intensity 1, 3 slices and 6 steps per direction. Rust clamps
slices to 1–8 and steps to 1–16; the C API rejects values outside these ranges.
AO defaults off to preserve existing scene output and render tests.

`ctx.scene3d.post.contact_shadows` adds contact shadows in the same pass: each
pixel steps toward the sun across the depth buffer, up to that many world
units, and loses the sun if something on screen sits in front of a step.
They catch the small, sharp shadows the shadow map is too coarse for:
under feet, pebbles and cups, and in creases. 0.3 suits people and props;
0 (the default) is off. Like AO they only see what is on screen.

`ctx.scene3d.post.anti_aliasing` chooses `AntiAliasing::None` (default), `Fxaa`,
`Smaa` (SMAA 1x) or `Taa` (temporal, below). FXAA and SMAA run on
tone-mapped LDR before sprites and UI,
in addition to the configured geometry MSAA. FXAA is cheaper; SMAA searches
edges and uses the reference area/search lookup textures (MIT licensed,
notices in `src/render/smaa/`).

`Post::ao_half_resolution` traces at half width and height and resolves to
full resolution with depth-aware weights. Contact shadows remain sharp:
their march runs at full resolution during the resolve in this mode.
`Post::ao_temporal` rotates slice directions over eight frames and reprojects
history using the previous camera matrix, independently of TAA. Depth mismatch,
sky and offscreen reprojections reject history; a local clamp limits trails.
It uses the same object motion vectors as TAA (below), so moving models
reproject with their own motion.
Both switches default off. High uses half-resolution temporal AO; Ultra does too
with more samples. Lower presets leave AO off.

GTAO uses one RG8 trace target (full or half resolution), one full-resolution
output and two fragment passes. Temporal mode adds two full-resolution RGBA16F
history targets; spatial mode allocates no history. With SSR the full-resolution
output is the first mip of the shared image; its reflection mip is untouched.
Enabling it also enables the solid depth prepass on GPUs that normally skip
that prepass. It is skipped on WebGL2/downlevel devices without compute
capabilities, matching the engine's optional advanced-effects fallback.
FXAA and SMAA use fragment passes, including on WebGL2: FXAA adds one LDR
intermediate and one pass, SMAA adds three passes and edge/weight targets.
Targets are reused until resize. No timing claim is made without a measured
scene. Screen-space AO cannot see offscreen or hidden occluders and can halo
at silhouettes. Target cameras and transparent surfaces do not receive AO.
History resets on resize, mode changes and AO sample/radius/intensity changes.
Use fewer slices/steps with temporal mode to offset reprojection and bandwidth costs.

Offscreen release measurement on Apple M4 Max (Metal), 1920×1080, a floor and
2,501 boxes, no contact shadows or TAA: full-resolution 4 slices × 8 steps
**2.35 ms**, half-resolution 4 × 8 **0.76 ms**, half-resolution temporal 2 × 4
**0.36 ms** for the two AO passes combined. These are means of the final 20
of 80 frames per mode from the engine GPU timestamp profiler, excluding scene
rendering. They measure cost, not equal image quality; results depend on the
scene and GPU. Reproduce with
`cargo test --release --features 3d --lib ao_gpu_timings_1080p -- --ignored --nocapture`.

### Temporal antialiasing

`AntiAliasing::Taa` (`anti_aliasing::taa` in C++) spreads the work of
antialiasing over frames. Each frame the camera is nudged by a sub-pixel
amount (a 16 position Halton sequence), so every frame samples the picture
at slightly different spots; the lit HDR frame is then blended with the
last result, moved along each pixel's motion. It runs on the HDR frame
before bloom, screen effects and tone mapping, so bright edges do not
flicker and bloom stays steady, and it also smooths shading and thin
details that FXAA and SMAA cannot see. The geometry MSAA stays on under it.

Motion comes from two places. Anything that did not move relative to the
world is found from the depth buffer and the last frame's camera. Models
whose transform changed since the last frame (tracked per entity, while
TAA or temporal AO is on) are drawn again, right after the depth prepass,
into a small two-channel motion image: how much further the model moved on
screen than the camera alone explains, worked out from the model matrix it
had the frame before. Only models that moved are drawn there, so a still
scene costs nothing extra. Both TAA and temporal AO (`ctx.scene3d.post.ao_temporal`)
add it to the camera's motion, taking it from the nearest surface in each
3x3 block of pixels so the edges of moving models follow them. Where the
history disagrees with the colors around a pixel (something moved into
view, a light changed), it is clipped to them, so there are no trails
behind moving models. The result is softened a little, so
`ctx.scene3d.post.taa_sharpness` (default 0.25, 0 to 1) sharpens it back.

The history starts over by itself on window resizes, render scale
changes, when TAA or another screen effect is turned on or off, and when
the camera jumps far or turns sharply in one frame. After a camera cut the
engine cannot see (a teleport that keeps the angle, a switch to a new
scene), or after teleporting an object across the screen, call
`ctx.gfx.reset_temporal_history()`. It resets temporal AO and screen-space
reflections too, and a model teleported on that frame has no motion. A
reset asked for on a frame that draws nothing (a loading frame) applies to
the next frame that does.

To drop the motion of one model and keep the rest of the picture's
history, call `ctx.gfx.reset_motion(entity)` (`gfx.reset_motion(entity)`
in C++) after teleporting it, or when its animation snaps to another pose
with no blend (`Animator::play`, a cut between clips): it then has no
motion on the next drawn frame, and the frame after measures from there.
For a level-of-detail group pass the group's entity, and for a mesh posed
through `SkinOf` the mesh's own entity.

second time as it was, on the compute and the CPU skinning paths alike,

mesh, another number of joints) has no motion on its first frame.

A material's `vertex` function moves vertices with its own motion too:
the motion pass runs it as of this frame and the last (see "Motion of
`vertex`" under [The shader contract](#the-shader-contract)), so swaying
grass and trees, waves and particles from a GPU buffer that keeps its last
frame write how far each corner moved. Models drawn with a shader whose
`vertex` reads `frame.time` or `items` are drawn into the motion image
every frame, even while their transform holds still; others only when
they moved. What it does not see: a `vertex` function animated by
material values or textures, unless it asks `drawing_last_frame()`, and
meshes drawn from a GPU buffer that doesn't keep its last frame, whose
elements move only with their entity. These rely on the color clipping

What to expect:

- **Cost.** Two passes: one draws the motion of the models that moved
  (depth-tested against the opaque pass, so it costs their vertices and
  nothing else; with nothing moved it only clears), one resolves the frame.
  Memory: three full-size HDR images (the lit frame and two histories, 8
  bytes a pixel each), a motion image (8 bytes a pixel) and its
  multisampled partner (8 bytes a sample, discarded after use on tiled
  GPUs); the opaque pass also keeps its depth for the motion pass. With TAA
  off none of this is allocated or drawn.
- **Skinned and morphing models** cost a second posing of each model
  whose pose changed (one more compute dispatch, or one more CPU posing
  and upload on the fallback), a second copy of its posed vertices, and
  the last frame's skin matrices and morph weights; the motion pass draws
  them with one more vertex buffer (the second posing writes positions
  only). With TAA and temporal AO off nothing is kept. Measured offscreen
  in release on an Apple M4 Max (Metal) with the rendering baseline's
  `animated` scene (400 figures of 16 joints, every skeleton moving every
  frame, 1280x720, `PONKO_RENDER_TAA=1`), against the same build with the
  pose history off, on a busy machine: the skinning pass took about 1 ms
  more GPU time (fastest frames 0.9 to 1.2 ms without, 2.0 to 2.1 ms
  with), the motion pass 0.08 ms, preparing the frame about 0.2 ms more
  CPU time, and 400 KB more skin matrices were uploaded a frame. Most of
  it is one more compute dispatch per moving model, so characters that
  hold a pose cost nothing.
- **Moving `vertex` functions** cost one more draw of their models in the
  motion pass every frame (their vertices, `vertex` run twice), whether
  they moved or not, and nothing in the other passes. Shaders whose
  `vertex` reads neither `frame.time` nor `items` cost nothing, nor does
  anything with TAA and temporal AO off. Measured offscreen in release on
  an Apple M4 Max (Metal) with the rendering baseline's `game-like` scene
  (1280x720, high quality, `PONKO_RENDER_WIND=1`: 30,000 trees swaying in
  their `vertex` and 4,096 cubes placed from a GPU buffer that keeps its
  last frame) on a busy machine, against the same build with
  `PONKO_RENDER_VERTEX_MOTION=0`: the motion pass took 0.20 ms instead of
  0.05 ms of GPU time with `PONKO_RENDER_TAA=1`, and the frame's CPU time
  did not change beyond the run-to-run noise (about 0.08 ms). At low quality (no TAA, no
  temporal AO) there is no motion pass and the two builds measure the same.
- **Particles, trails and transparent surfaces** have no motion of their
  own. They reproject with the opaque surface behind them, so a still
  one is clean and a fast one can ghost a little before the clipping
  catches it. A `vertex` function's own motion (wind, waves) is part of
  the motion of solid models, as above.
- **Meshes drawn from a GPU buffer** (see "GPU buffers") move with their
  entity: the model matrix of the last frame and of this one are applied to
  the buffer's elements. What the buffer does to each element between
  frames (a particle's own motion) is tracked only for a buffer that keeps
  its last frame (`set_gpu_buffer_motion(buffer, true)`): it takes twice the
  memory and one GPU copy of the buffer a frame while TAA or temporal AO is
  on (about 4 MB read and written for 262,144 elements; the copy is too
  short for this machine's pass timer to measure). Elsewhere a fast
  particle can ghost a little like the particles above.
- **LOD groups** have no motion of their own either (except skinned
  levels posed by the group's skeleton, which move like skinned models):
  they suit scenery that stays put. A moving object is best drawn as a
  `Model`.
- **Jitter** moves only the 3D camera. Shadow maps, sprites and UI, picking
  rays and anything you read back on the CPU use the unjittered camera;
  GTAO and screen effects see the same jittered depth as the frame, so they
  agree with it.
- **Resolution scale.** The resolve takes its input and output sizes
  separately, so a lower-resolution render can later be resolved up to
  the window's size; today both are the window's size.
- **WebGL2** cannot run it (it needs the compute-class devices that
  ambient occlusion needs, and reads the multisampled depth), so there
  `Taa` draws with FXAA instead, without any error.

### Screen-space reflections

`ctx.scene3d.post.ssr` (0 to 1; 0, the default, is off) makes glossy surfaces
reflect what is on screen: wet streets, polished floors, still water. Each
pixel follows its reflected view ray across the depth buffer, in 32
jittered steps that start short, then halves the step that went behind
something six times to find the hit. The ray reaches up to
`ctx.scene3d.post.ssr_max_distance` world units (default 30). The opaque pass uses
what it hit in place of the sky or reflection probe the surface would
reflect otherwise, with the surface's own Fresnel, so a dielectric floor
reflects mostly at grazing angles and a metal one everywhere. Reflections
stay at full strength up to roughness 0.4, then fade out by 0.7. Mirrors
stay sharp; glossy floors use progressively coarser,
depth-aware reflection mips, with interpolation between levels.

Where the ray finds nothing (it leaves the screen, passes behind
something, or runs out of length) the environment's reflection stays. The
reflection also fades out near the screen's edges and over the last 40% of
the ray, so it never cuts off hard.

So a mirror never shows what is off the screen or hidden behind something
on it. Ray-traced reflections would; `crates/ponko_bench/RAY_TRACING.md`
measures both and why the engine doesn't use hardware ray tracing yet.

What to expect:

- **Cost.** A half-resolution full-screen pass after the depth prepass
  (each of its pixels stands for a 2x2 block), and a copy of the opaque
  image after the opaque pass. One compute dispatch builds up to four
  smaller, depth-aware reflection levels from the trace, starting at
  quarter resolution; it reads the depth only where there is something to
  filter. The GPU profiler shows them as `screen reflections` (the trace)
  and `reflection blur` (on tile-based GPUs the latter overlaps the start
  of the opaque pass, which then carries most of its time). A release
  offscreen test (`reflection_filter_gpu_cost`, ignored by default), at
  1920x1080 on an Apple M4 Max with a glossy floor and an emissive box,
  measured about 0.28 ms for the trace and 0.05 ms of frame time for the
  levels (frames with and without them, alternated); a scene measurement,
  not a bound.
  The High and Ultra presets turn SSR on. Memory: the
  copy (8 bytes a pixel) and one image the opaque pass reads both the
  reflections and GTAO from (8 bytes a pixel, plus a quarter of that for
  the half-size reflections, plus under 1.4 bytes per full-size pixel for
  the filter levels and the image they are made in). Turning them on also
  turns on the solid depth prepass on GPUs that skip it. With `ssr` at 0 none of this is
  allocated or drawn.
- **Blur limits.** The footprint grows with roughness, up to about 32
  full-size pixels; it does not track ray-hit distance or sample a GGX
  cone. Depth-plane rejection reduces bleeding across receiver edges, but
  there is no normal buffer and coarse mips can still lose thin details.
  Confidence-premultiplied filtering fades misses toward the environment.
- **Last frame's colors.** The reflections are mixed in while the frame
  is lit, so the colors come from the last frame's image, at the point
  the hit was then. The first frame after they are turned on, after a
  resize or after a camera cut reflects only the environment, and a moving
  object's reflection trails it by a frame.
- **Only what is on screen** is reflected: not what is behind the camera,
  off to the side or hidden behind something else. A reflection fades out
  as what it shows leaves the screen.
- **Normals come from the depth buffer**, since the engine keeps no normal
  image: the reflection follows a surface's shape, but not its normal map.
- **Grain.** The steps are jittered per pixel and per frame. TAA averages
  that away; without it, reflections show some noise along their edges,
  and the half resolution makes those edges a little blocky.
- **Transparent surfaces and target cameras** don't get them; nor does
  WebGL2 (like GTAO, they need a compute-class device).

### Optional scene quality autotuning

Rust games can opt into `ctx.scene3d.set_quality_autotune(Some(policy))` for the
current scene. Default behavior is unchanged: the policy is absent. The
controller adjusts only render scale and the sun's shadow-map resolution;
it never selects effects, capability tiers, local-light settings or MSAA.
The policy, its freeze and the pins are scene settings (copied and replaced
with `ctx.scene3d`); the running controller is the context's, and
`ctx.quality_autotune_status()` reports it. This tested Rust example also
runs without a GPU. C/C++ APIs stay compatible; new native autotune entry
points are not provided yet.


**Precedence.** Calling `set_quality` pins both controlled settings, even
before enabling autotune and even if a value equals the default. Existing
non-default settings, explicit pins and settings outside policy bounds are
also protected. Enabling the policy delegates only the remaining defaults
to it. A direct field write differing from the last automatic value pins
that field for the policy's lifetime. Plain public fields cannot report
same-value assignments: use `ctx.scene3d.pin_render_scale` and `ctx.scene3d.pin_shadow_resolution`
for options-menu choices, including selecting an unchanged/default value.
Every other quality field remains entirely game-owned. Disabling keeps the
last choices; scene replacement disables autotune and the new scene must
opt in again. Use a fresh context to discard explicit user pins.

**GPU evidence and stability.** Only unsmoothed timestamp-query frame durations
count. Unsupported/disabled queries, zero/invalid samples, repeated samples,
and samples more than eight rendered frames or 250 ms old make no decisions.
CPU frame time, display wait and FPS are never substitutes. Capture frames,
headless runs, paused execution and active input scenarios freeze the policy;
freeze explicitly before a whole deterministic screenshot sequence, including
warm-up, so earlier live frames cannot influence it.

A reduction requires 30 consecutive fresh samples above 110% of the GPU
budget; restoration requires 180 below 75%. The dead band and missing data
clear the evidence run. Every decision waits another 120 fresh samples before
collecting evidence again. Reductions step scale down by at most 0.05 to its
floor, then halve sun-map resolution to its floor. Restoration doubles sun
maps first, then increases scale by at most 0.05. One setting changes per
decision. Before an upgrade, the measured GPU time multiplied by the squared
size ratio must also fit within 90% of budget. This conservative estimate
avoids repeatedly doubling a shadow map that would immediately exceed budget;
insufficient headroom is exposed as a waiting reason. Policy bounds are
sanitized to engine limits (scale 0.25–2,
sun maps 256–4096); reversed ceilings become the floor. Target GPU time is
clamped to 1–1000 ms. Untunable starting values stay pinned. These are gradual
quality controls, not a guarantee of frame rate for every scene.

Scale changes request the central temporal-history reset before scene
preparation; resized targets also invalidate history through the normal
prepare path. `ctx.quality_autotune_status()` and the additive
`perf.get.render_inspector.quality_autotune` object expose the sanitized policy,
active choices, pins, explicit freeze flag, current reason, decision count and
last 16 decisions (oldest first, with sample sequence, GPU milliseconds,
reason and old/new values). The inspector field is `null` when disabled or
without `3d`; schema remains 1. State updates allocate nothing in steady state;
requesting a status/history snapshot allocates on demand.

**Render scale.** `ctx.scene3d.post.render_scale` (0.25 to 2) draws the 3D scene
at that fraction of the window's size in each direction, then scales it to
the window, under the sprites, text and UI, which stay at the window's own
resolution. Everything in the 3D frame follows the scaled size: the
multisampled color and depth, bloom, ambient occlusion, antialiasing and
screen effects, so the cost of a pixel-bound scene drops about with the
square of the scale. The scaling pass is bilinear when the scene is
smaller than the window; above 1 (supersampling) it averages four taps.
`sharpen` (0 to 1) adds an adaptive contrast filter after the scaling,
which brings back some of the edge contrast a low scale loses (the idea of
AMD's CAS, in the same pass). At a scale of exactly 1 there is no extra
target and no extra pass.

**What switching costs.** Nothing is drawn from stale targets: the next
frame makes what it needs first.

- A new `render_scale`, or turning AO or FXAA on or off, makes the
  frame's targets again (one frame of allocation, no more).
- A new `shadow_resolution` or `shadow_cascades` makes the sun's shadow
  map again; `light_shadow_atlas` the lights' atlas.
  Turning every shadow off frees both.
- Turning SMAA off frees its targets.
- Turning global illumination on makes its probes (they start dark and
  fill in over the next frames); turning it off frees them.
- Changing the AO's `radius`, `intensity`, `slices` or `steps`, the
  `sharpen` amount or the shadow distance allocates nothing.

The old GPU memory is freed once the commands that used it have run. MSAA
(`ctx.scene3d.post.msaa`, 4 or 2 samples) is not part of the presets. Set in
a setup callback, it applies right after that callback, before the first
frame; set before any 3D asset is loaded (in its own, earlier callback),
the 3D pipelines are built only once. A change while the game runs makes
the 3D targets and pipelines again at the next frame, keeping meshes,
materials and shaders, and restarts temporal history. Any count other
than 2 or 4, or one the GPU can't take, keeps the current one; it is said
once on stderr and shows as the `multisampling` fallback below.

### What this GPU can do

Some effects need more than every GPU has: compute shaders (missing on
WebGL2), storage buffers, extra texture slots or timestamp queries. Where
the device lacks one, the renderer uses a fallback instead of failing, and
`ctx.gfx.gpu_capabilities()` says which: the adapter, the device limits these
choices depend on, and for each `GpuFeature` whether it is `available`, the
`fallback` used and the `reason`. It is decided once, when the window
opens, and is `None` in headless runs. The report serializes to JSON for
tools.

```rust
use ponko::prelude::*;

fn startup(ctx: &mut Ctx) {
    if let Some(caps) = ctx.gfx.gpu_capabilities() {
        if !caps.available(ponko::render::GpuFeature::TemporalAntialiasing) {
            // FXAA will be drawn instead; maybe pick SMAA yourself.
        }
    }
}
```

From C, `ponko_ctx_gpu_capabilities` writes the same report as JSON
(`PONKO_NOT_FOUND` before a GPU opens), and `ponko_ctx_gpu_tier` and
`ponko_ctx_gpu_feature` answer for the tier and one feature; in C++,
`ctx.gfx.gpu_capabilities()`, `ctx.gpu_tier()` and `ctx.gpu_has(feature)`.

| feature | needs | without it |
|---|---|---|
| `timestamps` | `TIMESTAMP_QUERY` | no GPU pass times; `RenderStats::gpu_ms` is `None` |
| `compute` | compute shaders and storage textures | the effects below that need them are skipped |
| `storage_buffers` | storage buffers in shaders | `GpuBuffer`s do nothing; `items` shaders use the built-in one |
| `compute_culling` | compute, 4 storage buffers | instance batches are culled on the CPU, cell by cell, and drawn with direct draws (the same picture) |
| `gpu_skinning` | compute, 6 storage buffers | skinned and morphed meshes are posed on the CPU |
| `occlusion_culling` | compute | off |
| `temporal_antialiasing` | compute | FXAA |
| `ambient_occlusion`, `contact_shadows`, `screen_space_reflections` | compute | off |
| `global_illumination` | compute, 18 sampled textures | off |
| `decals` | 17 sampled textures | projected decals are not drawn |
| `multisampling` | the `post.msaa` count for the 3D targets; in a browser's WebGL2 none (wgpu can't make multisampled textures there) | 4x, or none in a browser's WebGL2 (turn on FXAA or SMAA) |

### GPU tiers

`GpuTier` sorts GPUs into three classes by what every device of the class
draws, from the weakest the engine runs on. `caps.tier` is the tier a
device's limits reach. A tier is a floor: a device may have more than its
tier's minimum, and the per-feature entries above stay the exact answer.

| | `webgl2` | `baseline` | `desktop` |
|---|---|---|---|
| typical devices | WebGL2 in a browser, GLES 3.0 | WebGPU in a browser, GLES 3.1, D3D11-class, mobile | Vulkan, Metal and D3D12 desktop and laptop GPUs |
| floor (`GpuTier::minimum_limits`) | wgpu's `downlevel_webgl2_defaults`: no compute | wgpu's `downlevel_defaults`: compute, 4 storage buffers, 2048² textures | WebGPU's defaults with 18 sampled textures and 8 storage buffers |
| compute, storage buffers, GPU and occlusion culling | no | yes | yes |
| TAA, ambient occlusion, contact shadows, screen-space reflections | no | yes | yes |
| GPU skinning | no | if the device has 6 storage buffers | yes |
| decals, global illumination | no | if the device has 17 / 18 sampled textures | yes |
| GPU timestamps | depends on the device | depends on the device | depends on the device |
| `set_recommended_quality` | low, with FXAA | medium | high |

Unavailable at each tier's floor, with what is drawn instead (the same
words the report's `fallback` uses):

- **`webgl2`**: every feature but multisampling. TAA draws FXAA; ambient
  occlusion, contact shadows, screen-space reflections, occlusion
  culling and global illumination are off; static instance batches are
  culled on the CPU, cell by cell (the same picture); skinned and morphed meshes are posed on the CPU;
  `GpuBuffer`s and compute passes do nothing; material shaders that
  read `items` and GPU-buffer instance setup report a storage-capability
  error with a fix to use batches or entities; decals are not drawn; there are
  no GPU pass times. Shadow maps and the light shadow atlas are at most
  2048² (the renderer caps them at the device's texture size).
- **`baseline`**: GPU skinning (posed on the CPU), decals (not drawn),
  global illumination (sky and ambient light only) and timestamps.
- **`desktop`**: only timestamps, which no tier guarantees.

Nothing in the table is a GPU error on a weaker device: every preset,
Ultra included, draws on every tier, with the fallbacks above. To make the
settings say what is drawn (for an options menu),
`ctx.scene3d.set_recommended_quality(caps)` applies the preset meant for the
GPU in `caps` (the open one, `ctx.gfx.gpu_capabilities()`, or a tier's
floor, `GpuTier::report()`), and `ctx.scene3d.fit_quality_to(caps)` takes
out of the current settings what that GPU lacks (TAA becomes FXAA; AO,
reflections and GI go off; shadow maps shrink to the largest texture).
Ultra is never picked for you: its 512 MB of shadow maps is the player's
choice.

## Terrain

Ground that goes on for kilometers is made of tiles: squares of
heightfield terrain, each from a grid of heights.
`ctx.gfx.create_terrain_tile(&heights, size, material)` makes one from n × n
heights, row after row along +Z, and you spawn it with a `d3::Transform`
at its corner instead of a `Model`. Neighbouring tiles share their edge
heights, so a tile of 65 × 65 heights 2 m apart is 128 m wide and the
next one starts 128 m further on. A NaN height cuts a hole, for a cave
mouth or a tunnel.

Each tile is drawn more coarsely as the camera moves away: every height
up to `lod_distance` (by default the tile's size), then every 2nd height
to twice that, every 4th to four times that, and every 8th beyond. The
lighting doesn't jump between levels, because the normals always come
from every height. A short skirt hangs down around each tile's edge, so
there are no cracks where tiles of different detail meet.

## Levels of detail

A `LodGroup` draws cheaper meshes as an object shrinks on screen, and none
once it's small enough not to matter. It goes on an entity with a
`d3::Transform` in place of a `Model`. Its levels go from the most detailed
down, each with the fraction of the screen's height the object must cover
for it to show (as in Unity, so the choice doesn't change with the window
size), and the object's `size`, the height that counts. `LodLevel::at_distance`
and `LodGroup::distance_at` convert between distances and those fractions
for games that think in metres.

- **Crossfades.** A level with a `fade_width` hands over to the next one
  across the top of its range instead of all at once. For that stretch both
  levels draw through matching dither patterns, so every pixel shows one
  or the other and no hole opens between them. `ctx.scene3d.lod.crossfade = false`
  turns every fade into a plain switch, which costs less. The first
  crossfade a shader draws builds its dithering pipelines, several
  milliseconds inside that frame; `ctx.gfx.warm_render_pipelines()` (C++ the
  same) at the end of loading builds them up front, and
  `render_stats().pipelines_built` counts any still built while drawing
  (`pipeline_stats().misses` since the start).
- **Quality.** `ctx.scene3d.lod.bias` scales every group's screen height: 2 keeps
  detail twice as far away, 0.5 drops it at half the distance. It is the
  setting a graphics quality menu changes.
- **Shadows.** The shadow maps use the level the main camera picked, crossfade
  included. `with_shadow(LodShadow::Casters(...))` gives a level other meshes
  to cast with (a simple stand-in, or the detailed meshes while a billboard
  shows); an empty list casts no shadow.
- **Holding a level.** A `LodHysteresis(0.1)` on the group's entity keeps
  the level shown until the group is 10% past that level's range, so a
  camera resting at a threshold doesn't flip between two levels every
  frame. It matters most for levels that switch at once; crossfades pass
  through. Each camera keeps its own choice. In scene files it's
  `"LodHysteresis": 0.1`.
- **Animated models.** A `Skeleton` on the group's entity poses its skinned
  renderers, at every level and through crossfades, so a character can have
  cheaper meshes bound to the same skeleton. Every level's joint matrices
  are worked out each frame (a few per mesh), but only the levels a view
  draws are skinned on the GPU. The group must hold the skeleton itself:
  `SkinOf` doesn't reach a group's renderers.
- **Occlusion culling.** Each renderer of the level shown is culled like a
  model. With the camera moving, the test allows for how far it moved since
  the depth it tests against, so what comes into view is drawn the same
  frame.
- **Other cameras.** Cameras drawing into render targets pick their own
  levels.
- **Decals.** A `ReceivesDecals` on the group's entity applies to every
  level, as it does to a `Model`.
- **Scene files.** A group is saved as `"LodGroup": { "size": 8, "levels":
  [{ "screen_height": 0.3, "renderers": [{ "mesh": "tree.glb" }] }] }`. Its
  meshes are loaded with the scene, and a group with a mesh made in code
  isn't saved.

Picking runs in parallel across all groups each frame, and the meshes it
picks are drawn instanced with everything else, so thousands of groups
cost about what their visible meshes do. Past that, the picking itself
shows: on the machine measured, 0.5 to 2 ms of CPU a frame for 10,000 to
100,000 groups, 17 ms or more for a million.

cheaper of the two for level batches of 10,000 to a million copies: the
GPU pass saved at most 0.3 ms of CPU and cost 0.15 to 3.9 ms more GPU time
(0.15 to 0.47 ms at 10,000 to 100,000 copies, 3.9 ms at a million).
So `BatchCulling::Auto` keeps a batch of several levels on the CPU at any
size; `PreferGpu` still sends it to the GPU. Other GPUs, or much heavier
meshes, may make the GPU pass worth it: measure before choosing it. A
device without compute culling always picks on the CPU, with the same
bands.

From C, `ponko_lod_instance_levels` takes the group's size, center and
levels (as for `ponko_ctx_set_lod_group`), the scale, the field of view and
the `PonkoLodSettings`, and returns `PONKO_NOT_FOUND` for a group a batch
can't draw; C++ `ponko::lod_instance_levels` returns `std::nullopt` then.
`ponko_ctx_set_lod_hysteresis` and `ponko_ctx_lod_hysteresis` (C++
`set_lod_hysteresis`, `lod_hysteresis`) set and read a group's
`LodHysteresis`; 0 is none.

## Occlusion culling

In a town or a forest, most of what's in view is behind something: a
house, a hill, a trunk. With occlusion culling on, those models aren't
drawn.

- **How.** After the depth pass, a compute pass shrinks the depth to a grid
  of tiles (about 160 across) holding each tile's farthest depth. The CPU
  reads it back without waiting for the GPU, so the grid it culls with is
  one to three frames old. A model is skipped when its bounding box, seen
  from where the camera was then, lies behind that depth everywhere it
  covers. The test is a handful of lookups per model, done on all cores
  with the rest of the culling.
- **Late by a frame.** Something that comes out from behind a wall can show
  up a frame or two late. Turning the camera doesn't cause this (the
  grid is tested from the view it was drawn from). Moving it can, so the
  box is grown by how far the camera moved since that frame. A cut to a new
  place is safe: nothing far from the old view is culled.
- **Shadows.** Hidden models still cast shadows into the view.
- **What counts as an occluder.** Everything solid that the camera
  drew, including cut-out leaves (only their opaque texels). Transparent
  surfaces and particles hide nothing.
- **Cost and limits.** Making the grid reads each depth sample once, and
  testing a model costs far less than drawing it. `ctx.perf.render_stats().occluded`
  counts the models skipped last frame. Needs compute shaders: on WebGL2
  nothing is culled. Cameras that draw into render targets don't cull.

## Streaming big worlds

A world bigger than memory loads around the player and frees what's far
behind. The engine gives three pieces for this, and the game decides what
a "cell" holds: terrain, trees, houses, or all of them.

- `CellStreamer::new(cell_size, load_radius, keep_radius)` keeps a grid
  of square cells on the ground. Each frame, `cells.update(position,
  max_new)` works out which cells came within `load_radius` (at most
  `max_new` of them, nearest first, so the work spreads over frames) and
  which went beyond `keep_radius`. Read them with `to_load()` and
  `to_drop()`. The gap between the two radii stops a cell at the edge from
  loading and dropping over and over as the camera moves back and forth.
- `Job::spawn(|| ...)` runs work on a worker thread, and `job.take()`
  hands its result over once it's done, without waiting. Build the data
  there (heights, `MeshData`, decoded files) and keep GPU work on the
  game's thread. `TerrainTileData::new(&heights, size)` builds every
  detail level of a tile, and `ctx.gfx.upload_terrain_tile(&data, material)`
  is then the quick part. In C++, use `std::async` for the work.
- `ctx.gfx.free(mesh)` and `ctx.gfx.free(tile)` give the GPU
  memory back (`ctx.free(h)` does the same for any handle; see
  [Handles](handles.md)). Despawn the entity that showed it too: until
  then it draws nothing. Drop the handle: the next mesh made takes its
  slot (under a new handle), so streaming doesn't grow the mesh tables. To hide a model for a while (a
  plant beyond its draw distance), show `Mesh::NONE` (C++
  `ponko::mesh_none`) instead: it draws nothing, and the real mesh can be
  shown again.
  Reloading an evicted glTF keeps its existing materials and textures,
  including custom shader edits, so repeated travel does not allocate a
  fresh material set every time geometry comes back.
- A `FarTerrain` draws the rest of the world beyond the loaded tiles, from
  a coarse grid of heights you give it (the world's heights every 32 m,
  say): a few large meshes with the terrain material. When a tile is
  shown, `far.cut(cell)` cuts the far ground away under it, and
  `far.uncut(cell)` fills it in again when the tile goes; `far.update(ctx)`
  rebuilds only the meshes around a changed cell. The far ground never
  overlaps a tile, so nothing fights over depth, and a skirt hangs down
  from every cut edge, beside the tile's own skirt, so there's no crack
  where coarse and fine ground meet. Its grid must line up with the cells:
  the cell size a whole number of its spacing, its corner on its grid.

## Mixing with 2D

3D draws first and everything 2D draws on top: sprites, shapes, text, UI
and the inspector. A 3D game can have a 2D HUD with no extra work.
`ctx.gfx.clear(color)` sets the background behind both (behind 3D only with
a flat sky: the atmosphere draws its own).

## Cost

Models with the same mesh are drawn together in one instanced draw call.
A depth prepass finds the nearest surface of every pixel first, so each
pixel is lit only once, however many models overlap it.
Models outside the camera's view are skipped, except when they cast a
shadow into it. Edges are smoothed with 4x multisampling;
`ctx.scene3d.post.msaa = 2` takes 2 samples instead, about a third less GPU time
for the 3D passes on a large screen. (In a browser that has only WebGL2,
the 3D frame has no multisampling: set `ctx.scene3d.post.anti_aliasing` to FXAA or
SMAA.)

On GPUs that draw in tiles (Apple's), solid surfaces skip the prepass: those
GPUs already drop hidden solid fragments before lighting them, and only
cut-out ones (leaves, grass) gain from being laid down first.

On an M4 Max MacBook Pro, 100,000 spinning cubes with shadows run at
402 FPS, fullscreen with vsync off: 2.5 ms of CPU a frame, in 28 draw
calls. The same scene in Unity 6, made of GameObjects, runs at 54 FPS
([the comparison](performance.md#3d-ponko-vs-unity)). `ctx.gfx.models_drawn()` counts the
models drawn last frame, and `ctx.perf.render_stats()` gives the frame's draw
calls and CPU time.

## Particles

and queued bursts share the hard `max_particles` cap. Excess emission is discarded.
The same seed and frame steps reproduce an emitter's simulation independently of
other emitters. This is a visual frame simulation, not a fixed-tick lockstep effect.

(C++: `ctx.particle_burst(entity, 20)`).

particles spawn at the end of the current frame; existing particles advance first.

World-space particles keep their spawn transform. With `ponko::particles::ParticleSpace::Local`,
existing particles follow the entity, gravity uses its coordinates, and the largest
absolute scale component scales their square billboards. World-space speed uses
rotation without scale. Supply `texture: Some(texture)` for sprites; `sheet: (columns,
rows)` animates a row-major sprite sheet over each particle's lifetime. Without a
texture, particles are white quads tinted by their gradient.

Particles draw after opaque surfaces, depth tested without depth writes, one
instanced draw per live emitter. Emitter centers are sorted by camera-forward depth back to front, and
alpha particles are also sorted within each emitter. Overlapping emitters cannot
interleave their particles perfectly. Additive particles skip internal sorting.
These unlit billboards pass through HDR post processing. This first implementation
renders particles in the main camera only. Soft intersections are omitted: the
combined opaque/transparent pass does not expose depth for simultaneous sampling.
`run_headless` skips the visual frame phase, so emitters remain idle without GPU work.

## Decals

There are two kinds. **Mesh decals** are built once on the CPU and drawn as a
mesh; **projected decals** are boxes the GPU paints onto whatever is inside
them each frame. The next section helps choose.

### Mesh decals

`MeshData::decal(projector, lift)` cuts the part of a mesh inside a box out as
a mesh of its own, with an image projected straight onto it: bullet holes,
scorch marks, splats, signs and tyre marks. `projector` places the box in the
mesh's space; it maps the unit cube (-0.5 to 0.5 on each axis) onto the box,
so its scale is the decal's size and depth. The image is projected along the
box's -Z (looking from +Z) with its top along +Y, and only faces turned
toward +Z get it. Every corner is lifted `lift` meters along its normal so
the decal sits on the surface without flickering; a few millimeters is
typical. The result is empty when the box misses the mesh.

### Projected decals

A `d3::Decal` is a box in the world whose image the renderer paints onto the
opaque surfaces inside it, every frame, with no mesh built. Footprints, blood,
tyre marks, bullet holes and dirt can number in the hundreds or thousands, and
they follow whatever is under them: a decal stays put while a model, a skinned
character or a door moves through its box, and a decal parented to a moving
model (see attachments) carries its mark along.

The entity's `d3::Transform` places the box (its scale multiplies `size`). As
with mesh decals the image is projected along the box's **-Z** (looking from
+Z), its top along +Y. In the example the box's +Z is turned to point up, so
the image lands on the ground below.

What a `d3::Decal` sets:

- **`image`, `normal_image`, `uv_min`, `uv_max`**: the layer of the decal
  texture array with the color image and the one with a tangent-space normal
  map (`d3::Decal::NO_IMAGE` for none; a decal without an image is a flat
  `color` rectangle), and the part of the layer to use, so a layer can be an
  atlas. Leave a few pixels of padding between atlas entries: mipmaps blend them.
- **`color`, `opacity`**: a tint on the image, and how much of it shows.
- **`roughness`, `metallic`**: the surface's values under the decal;
  negative leaves them. Wet blood is smooth, dust rough, dirt on metal not metallic.
- **`angle_fade_start`, `angle_fade_end`**: the decal fades out on surfaces
  turned away from its projection, so it doesn't stretch over a wall it
  grazes. Fully visible up to about 57°, gone at about 83° by default.
- **`depth_fade`**: how much of the box's depth the decal fades over at each
  end, so it doesn't end in a hard slice where the ground rises or falls.
- **`max_distance`**: past it the decal isn't drawn (it fades over the last
  quarter): the cheapest way to keep a level full of marks light.
- **`order`**: decals with a higher order draw over lower ones.
- **`layers`** and **`ReceivesDecals`**: a decal shows on a model when its
  `layers` and the model's `ReceivesDecals` share a bit (16 layers).
  Models without the component take every decal; `ReceivesDecals::NONE` opts
  a character or a first-person weapon out.

Projected decals are blended into the surface before lighting. They are lit
like the surface they land on, so they darken in shadow, take the sun, point
lights and reflections, and a normal map or lowered roughness catches the
light. Transparent materials, particles and the sky don't get decals.

**How it works and what it costs.** The renderer already sorts point and spot
lights into a grid of 16 x 9 screen cells with 24 slices of depth. Decals
share that grid: each frame the decals the camera sees (at most
`d3::Decal::MAX`, 4096; beyond that the nearest are kept) are listed per cell, and
a pixel blends the decals in its cell. A cell holds at most
`d3::Decal::MAX_PER_CLUSTER` (64), keeping the highest `order`s. Pixels outside
every decal pay one cell lookup. Collecting 4096 decals took 0.33 ms of CPU in
a release build, and an offscreen 1280 x 720 frame with 1000 to 4000 mostly
separate decals added roughly 1 to 3 ms (readback included) on a Metal GPU, so cost grows with
how many decals overlap each pixel, not with the geometry under them. Each
decal uses 128 bytes of GPU memory; the images are the texture array you set.

**WebGL2.** The mesh shader needs one more sampled texture for the decal images
than WebGL2 (and GPUs left at the default limit) allow. Native builds ask the
adapter for more; where that fails, `ctx.gfx.supports_decals()` is false and decals
are skipped cleanly: nothing else changes and no shader errors.

**Mesh decals or projected decals?** Use a projected decal for the many
small marks that appear and disappear while the game runs (footprints, blood,
bullet holes), for anything on moving or skinned models, on terrain, or on
geometry you can't cut (any model in the scene): nothing is built, so
spawning one is just spawning an entity. Use a mesh decal when the mark is
permanent and the GPU has no room for projected ones (WebGL2), when the decal
needs a material of its own (a glowing sign, an animated shader, glass), when
it must be transparent or sorted like a transparent surface, or when it
should follow one mesh exactly (a sign painted on a wall) and cost nothing
per pixel.

## Sky IBL and local reflections

Ambient light uses diffuse irradiance and a GGX-prefiltered environment with
roughness levels and a split-sum BRDF lookup. The diffuse part comes from
order-2 spherical harmonics of the environment (nine coefficients, projected on
the GPU from every texel of the cube), so it is smooth, with no sampling noise,
and looked up from a small cube like before. The procedural sky is baked when
its lighting or camera altitude changes. Without an atmosphere, the existing
sky and ground colors supply the environment. `ambient_intensity` scales the
sky environment; direct sun lighting is unchanged. Smooth and metallic
surfaces now show the sky in their reflections instead of a flat tint, and
rough ones are lit by the sky's direction and color, not just up and down.

A reflection probe captures six 128² linear HDR views only when requested.
Place its entity inside a room, then request a bake after creating the room.
The capture includes opaque geometry, emissive materials, sky and existing
lights; transparent objects are omitted, and where nothing is drawn the sky
shows. Captures use sky IBL to avoid recursive local reflections. Moving a probe
or changing the scene requires another bake. A bake asked for while the window
is hidden happens on the next frame that is drawn.

<div class="lang-pair">

```rust
# use ponko::prelude::*;

let volume = ponko::d3::ReflectionProbe {

    ..Default::default()
};
let probe = ctx.world.spawn((ponko::d3::Transform::at(vec3(0.0, 2.0, 0.0)), volume));
ctx.gfx.bake_reflection_probe(&mut ctx.world, probe)?;
// To use a file instead: ctx.gfx.try_load_reflection_probe(&mut ctx.world, probe, "room.exr")?;
# Ok(()) }
```

`d3::ReflectionProbe` defaults to a 10-unit axis-aligned box, a 1-unit fade
inside its boundary, intensity 1 and box projection enabled. Transform scale
and rotation do not change the box. Box projection intersects the reflection
ray with its walls before sampling; disable it for distant environments.
Where volumes overlap, the two smallest that reach a pixel are used: a room
inside a bigger one wins in its interior, and fades into the bigger one (or the
sky) across its `blend_distance`. Sky lighting fills any remaining weight;
outside all volumes, only the sky contributes.
Unbaked probes have no influence. `content` is context-owned: leave it zero
until baking or loading assigns it.

A context supports four resident probe environments. Repeated bakes reuse the
same slot; attempting to allocate a fifth returns an error. A probe can instead
load a linear HDR or EXR 2:1 equirectangular image (+Y at the top, +X at the
center). Loading and runtime capture use the same prefilter. Images must have
finite radiance; negative values clamp to zero and values above 65504 clamp to
the half-float range.

The renderer stores cube faces and roughness levels in a 2D RGBA16F atlas,
using the existing sky binding. No extra sampled-texture binding, compute pass
or cube array is required: the same render-pass path supports WebGL2. The atlas
uses 12 MiB, the scratch cube 0.75 MiB, and each allocated capture's six targets
0.75 MiB (plus retained multisample/depth target-camera resources). The BRDF
lookup is generated once per renderer. Baking costs six scene views plus
64-sample diffuse/specular filtering; it is synchronous GPU work on the next
rendered frame, so request bakes during loading or at infrequent scene changes.

## GPU buffers and particles

A `GpuBuffer` holds a fixed-length array of `vec4<f32>` elements on the GPU.
Create it with a nonzero length and either no initial data (zeroed) or one
value per element. `write_gpu_buffer` replaces a checked range before the next
frame; it is useful for setup and occasional input, without per-particle CPU work.

A buffer compute shader defines `fn update(i: u32)`, reading and writing
`items[i]`. The engine dispatches one invocation per element in workgroups of
64, checking the last group's bounds. `frame` and `material` have the same
uniforms as texture compute, including `frame.time.y` (delta seconds).
`ComputePass::for_buffer` and texture passes share one ordered list; each
finishes before the next begins, before drawing. Invocations within a pass
have no ordering: avoid reading values other invocations write.

world axes. A particle emitter therefore follows its entity. The engine
uploads one instance record per entity, shared by all `count` instances, and
draws once per entity, so use one entity per emitter.

The entity is culled by the mesh's bounds, which don't know where the shader
puts the instances. `set_mesh_gpu_bounds(mesh, center, radius)` grows them to
also hold a sphere around the entity (in the entity's space) that covers the
whole simulation; call it again to replace the sphere. Disable occlusion
culling for shader-displaced geometry. Depth and shadow passes use the same
vertex shader. With temporal antialiasing or temporal AO, a moving entity
also writes its motion vector, so the whole effect reprojects with it instead
of trailing. Each element's own motion is tracked once the buffer keeps its
last frame: `set_gpu_buffer_motion(buffer, true)` doubles its memory (the
copy lives after its elements, starting equal to them, so turning it on adds
no motion) and copies the elements there once a frame, after drawing, while
TAA or temporal AO is on. The motion pass then reads each element as of the
last frame. A `write_gpu_buffer` counts as motion from where the last frame
drew the elements; to place them somewhere new without it, write them before
turning the copy on, or reset the temporal history.
`set_gpu_buffer_motion(buffer, false)` frees the copy. Buffers cost no CPU
work per frame.

`free_gpu_buffer` releases a buffer's GPU memory, for effects that come and
go. Its compute passes are removed from the context's list and meshes drawing
from it draw nothing until bound to another buffer. The next buffer made takes
its id, so drop the handle (see [Handles](handles.md)).

On devices without vertex storage buffers (including WebGL2), creating a
material shader that reads `items` returns a located error with a fix, and
`set_mesh_gpu_buffer` panics with the device requirement and CPU-backed
alternatives. Use persistent instance batches or ordinary model entities
instead. Buffer compute passes still skip unsupported devices. Headless
games retain handles and validate writes, so setup code can be tested
without a GPU.

## GPU-driven instances

For large static populations, create a persistent instance batch instead of one
entity per object. The mesh supplies its materials. Transforms upload when the
batch is created or updated; steady frames upload only view and occlusion data.
World-space cells reject groups before individual instances are tested. Large
batches, and batches of heavy meshes, are culled per instance in a compute pass
and drawn with indirect indexed draws in the normal material, depth and shadow
passes; other batches, and all batches on devices without compute culling, are
culled on the CPU and drawn directly (see [Which path a batch takes](#which-path-a-batch-takes)).
Both draw the same picture. Static instances use camera motion for TAA; they
do not need an object motion pass.

Use one batch per streamed region, and `ctx.gfx.free(batch)` when unloading it.

ordering within a batch is unspecified. Materials should be opaque or cutout;
transparent instances are not depth sorted. Meshes must outlive their batches.

### Distance levels and colours

`create_instance_batch_levels` shares transforms and colours across multiple
`InstanceLevel` meshes. Each level draws exactly while
`min_distance <= distance < max_distance`, using distance to the instance
**origin**, rather than its mesh bounds. Infinity is allowed as an upper bound.
Adjacent bands partition instances; overlapping bands draw both meshes, allowing
custom materials to dither a crossfade. Gaps draw nothing. Bounds-based batch
and cell rejection is conservative, including off-centre meshes and proxies.

Each level's `camera` flag enables main and target camera views; `shadows`
enables sun, point and spot shadow views. This permits separate shadow proxies,
including different shadow distance bands. The batch's `cast_shadows` option and
`set_mesh_shadows` must also allow shadows. Target cameras select using their own
position, local shadow views use the light position, and sun cascades use the
main camera. Banded casters preserve `Lighting::shadow_refresh`: far cascades
keep taking turns, and an instance crossing a shadow band can remain stale until
that cascade's next turn, just like a moving caster. After one refresh cycle,
all cascades use the new selection. Stationary content can be kept by the
renderer even on a cascade's turn. Batch updates and mesh shadow flag changes
invalidate cached content immediately. Only the main view uses occlusion culling.

Colours are sRGB, converted exactly like `Model::with_color`. An empty slice uses
`options.tint`; otherwise supply one colour per transform (a mismatch panics).
Custom material shaders can read the resulting `Vertex.color` / `Surface.color`.
`update_instance_batch_colors` replaces transforms and colours together; empty
colours, including `update_instance_batch`, restore the uniform tint. Level
meshes and bands remain fixed until the batch is freed and recreated.

The existing `create_instance_batch` retains its inclusive, bounding-sphere
`options.max_distance` test. It uses a separate legacy distance mode because
sphere distance cannot reproduce half-open origin bands. The levels API ignores
`options.max_distance`; each level supplies its own band.

### Which path a batch takes

The renderer picks one of two paths for a batch when it is created, updated,
or changed with `set_instance_batch_culling`. Automatic batches also reselect
immediately when a material changes its blend mode:

- **GPU-driven**: the device has compute culling (`GpuFeature::ComputeCulling`:
  compute shaders and four storage buffers), and the batch has one level
  and either at least 131,072 instances or its instances times the
  triangles of its mesh reach 16,777,216 (4,096 instances of a
  5,000-triangle mesh do). A
  compute pass culls every such batch for the camera, every shadow view and
  every distance level, instance by instance. Each level is then drawn with
  one indirect draw per material.
- **CPU**: every other batch, and under the automatic choice every batch
  with a see-through (blended) material or with several distance levels,
  whatever its instances and triangles. The compute pass tests every
  instance for every level band, where the CPU skips whole cells: in the
  one scene measured (three levels, 480 triangles at the top, one GPU) it
  was slower at every size, see
  [Many copies that never move](#many-copies-that-never-move). For level
  meshes much heavier than that, measure; `PreferGpu` sends such a batch to
  the GPU. Both paths now preserve the batch's
  stable cell order, including exact depth ties; neither sorts transparent
  instances back to front. CPU cells are tested (frustum, distance bands, occlusion), and neighbouring
  visible instances are drawn together with direct draws from a vertex
  buffer. In a cell that a band's edge crosses, distances are tested per
  instance. A view's lists are kept while the view and the batch stay as
  they were.

Both paths draw the same instances, apart from those outside the view or
behind an occluder, which leave no pixels either way: the renderer's tests
compare the two on the same scenes, frame by frame while the camera moves
and after it jumps, with sun and local-light shadows, a late occlusion
grid, a camera drawing into a render target, cutout and see-through
materials and per-instance colours. Overlapping bands draw both meshes on
both paths.

The two thresholds are measured crossovers. A compute pass has a fixed cost
a frame, and a GPU-driven batch needs a bind group and an indirect draw per
level and pass; CPU culling costs by the cell, and draws whole cells. With
light meshes the CPU path is as fast or faster below about a hundred
thousand instances in one batch, so streamed regions of a few thousand
instances each stay on it, however many there are. With heavy meshes the
triangles saved by culling each instance are worth more than the pass.

**They were measured on one machine: an Apple GPU on Metal.** No other GPU,
driver or backend was measured, and yours may cross over elsewhere. The
numbers, and the command that makes them, are in
`crates/ponko_bench/STATIC_BATCHES.md`; run it on your target, and where the
automatic choice is wrong for a batch, say which path it takes:

### Limits, and what happens past them

`ctx.gfx.gpu_capabilities()`'s `instance_batches` (`GpuBatchLimits`) says what the
device allows before any batch exists, and `ctx.perf.render_stats().instance_batches`
(`InstanceBatchStats`) counts the batches on each path while the game runs.
Nothing here panics or fails GPU validation: a batch past a limit takes
the next path down. Anything not drawn is counted in the stats and warned
about once (in the terminal, or the browser's console on the web), again
only after the batch fitted in between.

| limit | from | past it |
|---|---|---|
| `gpu_driven` is `false` | no compute shaders, or fewer than four storage buffers (WebGL2) | every batch is culled on the CPU: `cpu_no_compute` |
| `max_gpu_instances` | one storage buffer binding (128 bytes an instance) | the batch is culled on the CPU: `cpu_over_limit` |
| `max_cpu_instances` | one GPU buffer (80 bytes an instance) | the batch is not drawn: `dropped`. Split it |
| a pool's record buffer is full | storage binding and buffer size | another pool is made for the next batches (`pools`); nothing is lost |
| a pool's views fill a page | storage binding and buffer size | the pool gets another page (`pages`, one more dispatch each); every view of every batch keeps its place, however many levels and shadow views there are |
| `max_cull_cells` | workgroups of one compute dispatch, per page | the batch views past it are not drawn that frame: `views_skipped`. Use bigger cells or fewer batches |
| an occlusion grid past the culling pass's depth buffer | the device's largest texture size (about 164,000 cells at 8192 px), less where one storage binding is smaller than that | GPU-driven batches skip occlusion culling (they draw more, never less); warned once |
| more views in a frame than the culling pass's buffer holds | one storage binding (112 bytes a view after the grid's room, and always room for 64: over a million views at 128 MiB) | GPU-driven batches are not drawn in the views past it: `views_skipped` |

Each view stores the visible list, temporary local ranks, and prefix counters:
about 8 bytes per instance plus 4 bytes per cell and per group of 64 cells.
Views that outgrow one page spill into another; all enabled levels remain drawn.

Reading the counts: `gpu_driven` + `cpu_small` + `cpu_no_compute` +
`cpu_over_limit` + `dropped` is `batches`. `cpu_small` is the normal state
of a scene of small regions. `cull_dispatches` is the pages with a view to
cull this frame (`pages`; 1 in most scenes). Each page records four compute
stages: local ranks, cell prefixes, group prefixes, and stable scatter.
`cull_jobs` counts culled batch/view/level combinations: it grows with shadow
views and levels, not with instances. `cpu_over_limit`, `dropped` and
`views_skipped` should be 0; if not, make the batches smaller. The same
counts are in the render inspector (`counts.instance_batches` in
`perf.get`).

### Memory

GPU-driven batches share pools: a buffer of records, and pages of two
buffers each (visible lists with the page's culling table, and draw
arguments) for the batches' views. All start at 64 KiB and double as
batches and views arrive; freed ranges are reused. A batch's records cost
`96 * instance_count + 32 * cell_count` bytes, once, whatever the number of
views. Each allocated **level/view** costs
`4 * (1 + 2 * instance_count + cell_count + ceil(cell_count / 64))` bytes
for its visible list and stable compaction scratch, and
`20 * mesh_part_count` bytes of draw arguments, plus a bind group.
The main term is **8 bytes per instance per level per view**. Sum only the
levels enabled for that view. They are allocated lazily for surviving
batches and kept at their high-water mark. Batches with no shadow levels
allocate none for shadow views. Each point light can add six views, each
spot light one, each sun cascade one, and each target camera one. The
frame's table costs 112 bytes a view (in the buffer the occlusion grid
uses) and 48 bytes a job (in its page).
`InstanceBatchStats::pool_bytes` is the pools' total, pages included.
A page whose views are all gone and a pool whose batches are all freed
give their buffers back after eight consecutive empty frames (each keeps a
64 KiB buffer or two for the next batch); `pools` and `pages` count the ones in
use. A pool or page that still holds something keeps the size it grew to.

A batch on the CPU path keeps `80 * instance_count` bytes of GPU vertex
records and one CPU list of visible runs per active level/view. CPU
transforms, colours and bounds remain available for explicit updates.

Neither path uploads transforms in steady frames, and preparing a steady
frame allocates nothing on either. Whole-batch rejection avoids culling
and draws outside the frustum or the widest enabled band; cells whose
bounds cannot intersect a level's band are rejected before frustum and
occlusion work. The compute pass is timed as `static instance cull` in
the GPU pass times. Animated, skinned and moving objects should continue
to use models.

## Global illumination

`ctx.scene3d.lighting.gi` turns on bounced light: sunlight, sky light and lamp
light that hits a surface and lights others from there. A red wall in the
sun tints the floor beside it red, a room lit through a window is lit all
over, and a closed room goes dark instead of taking the sky's ambient
light, all following the sun through a day and night. It is off by
default.

How it works: a grid of light probes moves with the camera, `extent`
across, one every `spacing` world units (the default 32 x 16 x 32 at 2
units is 17 x 9 x 17 probes). Each frame `probes_per_frame` of them look
around: each draws six small (16x16) views of what is near it, lit as
usual (the sun with its shadows, point and spot lights, the sky) and with
the probes' own light as the ambient light, so light bounces once more
each time a probe looks again. Each probe keeps how much light reaches it
from every direction, and how far away the surfaces around it are. A
surface then takes its ambient light from the eight probes around it,
each counted only as far as it can see the surface (from those
distances), which keeps light from leaking through walls. The probes
that look first are the new ones, nearest first (the grid moves a whole
spacing at a time, and keeps the probes that are still inside it), then
the ones that looked longest ago, nearer ones sooner.

Inside the grid the bounced light replaces the sky and ground ambient
light (and reflection probes' diffuse light); past its edges it fades back
to that over one spacing. Where it is darker than the sky's light would
be (indoors), reflections of the sky darken with it.

What to expect:

- **Cost.** Per frame: one more pass over the world to find what is near
  the grid, a 1024² shadow map of that, `probes_per_frame` times six small
  views, a compute pass, and eight probe lookups in every lit pixel. In a
  test scene of 900 buildings at 1280x720 on an Apple M4 Max, the defaults
  added about 0.5 ms of GPU time and 0.1 ms of CPU time a frame; 16 probes
  a frame added about 1 to 1.5 ms of GPU time. The Ultra preset turns it
  on. Memory: about
  2.5 kB per probe (6.5 MB for the default grid) and 2 MB for the views
  and their shadow map.
- **Lag.** Probes take turns, so bounced light catches up with a change
  (a door opening, a lamp turned on, the camera arriving somewhere new)
  over a few seconds at the defaults: 17 x 9 x 17 probes at 4 a frame is
  a full round in about 10 seconds at 60 frames a second, nearest probes
  first. Slow changes like the sun's are followed smoothly. More probes a
  frame catch up faster.
- **Coarse light.** It is diffuse light at the probes' spacing: it can't
  tell apart spaces much smaller than that, and walls thinner than about
  a tenth of the spacing may leak. Small things within a cell don't get
  their own bounced light, which is what ambient occlusion is for.
- **What the probes see.** Their views draw opaque surfaces near the grid
  (up to four spacings past it), not transparent ones or particles, with
  the coarser levels of detail, and not instance batches. Surfaces further
  away are sky to them. Point and spot lights light the views but cast no
  shadows in them.
- **Where it runs.** It needs compute shaders and an 18th texture slot in
  material shaders, which every desktop and laptop GPU has (Vulkan, Metal,
  DirectX 12). Elsewhere, as on WebGL2, it is skipped and the ambient light
  is as without it.
